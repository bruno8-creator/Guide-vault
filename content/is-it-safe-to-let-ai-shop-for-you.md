It is the week before Black Friday and you have a list: a TV for the living room, a stand mixer for your sister, a pair of running shoes in a size that always sells out. Instead of opening fifteen tabs, you type the list into ChatGPT or Gemini and ask it to find the best prices.

A few seconds later you get a tidy answer with prices, retailers and a "Buy" button. Some assistants will now go further and offer to place the order for you.

So is it safe to let AI shop for you? For a lot of shopping it works well enough. But ask one question before you tap "Buy": if the agent gets the price or the item wrong, whose problem is that?

Mostly yours, and store terms are increasingly written to say so. This guide covers where these tools slip and who pays when they do, then gives you a short check to run before any AI-found deal reaches your card.

>> **In short:** let AI pick the product, but check the price and the store yourself before any money moves.
>> - AI assistants are good at narrowing down *what* to buy, much less at *where it is cheapest right now*. In a Which? test, ChatGPT got the lowest price and retailer right for only 8 of 25 products.
>> - Several tools can now buy for you, from Google and Perplexity to store assistants at Walmart and Target. In almost every case the retailer, not the AI company, handles returns and refunds.
>> - Target's terms say purchases made by an AI agent you authorized count as purchases you authorized. "The bot did it" is not an excuse.
>> - If you already have a credit card and pay it off in full, it gives you stronger dispute rights than a debit card under US law. If you carry a balance, the interest can outweigh that advantage.
>> - Before an agent pays, check the seller's web address, the exact model number, the total with shipping and the price history.

## Researching vs buying: two very different jobs

"AI shopping" covers two activities that carry very different levels of risk.

The first is **research**. You describe what you need and the assistant comes back with suggestions, comparisons and links. OpenAI's [shopping research feature](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-shopping-research/), launched in November 2025, asks a few follow-up questions, then writes a comparison of options pulled from retail sites. If it is wrong, you lose a bit of time, and you still choose the store and enter your own card.

The second is **buying**, which the industry calls agentic checkout. Here the AI completes the purchase with a payment method you have saved, either the moment you approve or once a price you set is reached. A mistake at this stage costs real money, and fixing it means dealing with the retailer and sometimes your card issuer.

The line between them is easy to miss, so watch for one moment: when the assistant asks to use your saved card or your store account. **That is where research turns into buying.**

## How accurate is AI at finding the best price?

The most useful independent test so far comes from the UK consumer group Which?. In October 2026 it [asked ChatGPT to find the lowest price](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/can-you-get-a-good-deal-in-the-sales-using-ai-aJecz4r1AkR4) and the retailer offering it for 25 popular products, from laptops to vacuum cleaners, and compared the answers with what its own researchers found.

```chart
note: UK prices; Which? describes it as a snapshot analysis
Correct lowest price and retailer: 8
At least one issue: 17
```

That is about two answers in three with at least one problem. The kinds of error matter more, because each tells you what to check:

- **A wrong price for a real retailer.** In one case ChatGPT quoted £919.08 for a Microsoft Surface Pro at the Microsoft Store. The store's page said £999, and Amazon had it for £899.
- **An old price presented as current.** For a Lenovo Chromebook it gave £399, which turned out to be a six-month low. The cheapest current price Which? found at a reputable retailer was £599.
- **The wrong model.** Highlighted product boxes at the top of some answers often showed the wrong model or a higher price than the rest of the results.
- **Stale data from comparison sites.** Some prices seemed to come from price comparison sites that were out of date or inaccurate. In one case Which? could not find the TV for sale at the store named at all.

OpenAI told Which? that ChatGPT links to retailers so people can check prices themselves, and that it is working on refreshing prices faster. Its own [shopping research announcement](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-shopping-research/) also warns that the feature can get price and availability wrong.

The test covered one chatbot in one country at one moment, and other assistants have not been tested the same way. Still, the result is no surprise. Retail prices change constantly, sometimes several times a day during sales, and an AI answer can be no more current than whatever the tool last read.

Anyone who budgeted for that Chromebook at the chatbot's £399 would reach the store, find it at £599 and have to walk away or spend £200 more than planned. The number in the chat is a starting point.

The practical conclusion: **use AI to pick the product and treat its price as a lead to check.** Its view on which TV suits a bright room is often helpful. Its claim that a store has the lowest price this afternoon deserves the same suspicion as a stranger's tip.

The column worth a second look is the last one. ChatGPT, Perplexity, Copilot and Google describe the retailer as the "merchant of record": the store sells you the item, takes the payment and handles returns. If something goes wrong, you deal with the store, not the chatbot.

The announcement does not say what confirmation it asks for before paying, which is exactly the detail to look for when such a feature reaches your phone.

## Who pays when an AI agent gets it wrong

You ask an agent for "the 55-inch model" and it buys the 50-inch one, or two mixers arrive instead of one. Who carries that?

### What the store's terms say

Target was one of the first big US retailers to spell this out. Its [terms and conditions](https://www.target.com/terms) (last updated March 22, 2026) now include a section on "Agentic Commerce Agents". In plain terms:

- An agent can only act on your account if both you and Target have approved it. Within the permissions you grant, its actions are treated as actions you authorized.
- Target says it does not guarantee that an agent "will act exactly as you intend in all circumstances".
- Monitoring the agent is your job: flag any order you never approved quickly, to both Target and the agent.
- You can switch an agent off at any time in your Target account settings.

None of this means you are stuck with a mistaken order. According to [TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/111962-target-puts-customers-hook-ai-shopping-assistant-errors.html), a Target spokesperson told Business Insider that items bought through Gemini can still be returned or exchanged under the standard return policy. That promise is not in the terms themselves, and the purchase still counts as yours, so the usual return window and exclusions apply (often including opened electronics or clearance items).

Walmart adds more controls. Its [agent terms](https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2026/10/Making-It-Possible-for-Agents-to-Shop-on-Behalf-of-Customers), announced in October 2026, assume you sign off on each agent order yourself, though you can swap that for a budget ceiling (Walmart's examples are $50, $100 and $200). Agent orders appear apart from your other purchases, and if the agent gets it wrong, Walmart says the usual returns process still covers you.

>> **Watch out:** in most setups the chatbot passes your order to the store and its part is over. Your contract is with the merchant, and your safety net is the store's return policy plus your card's dispute rights.

## Your card's dispute rights: credit vs debit

If the store will not help, your card is the next line of defence, and the type of card matters.

With a **credit card**, the Fair Credit Billing Act lets you dispute billing errors, which the FTC says [include charges for goods you did not accept or that were not delivered as agreed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/using-credit-cards-and-disputing-charges), as well as unauthorized charges, where your liability is capped at $50. You need to write to the card issuer within 60 days of the first statement showing the charge. The issuer must acknowledge your letter within 30 days and resolve the dispute within 90.

For problems with the *quality* of what you bought, the CFPB explains that you may be able to [withhold payment on a credit card purchase](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-can-i-get-a-refund-on-a-product-or-service-i-purchased-with-my-credit-card-en-1969/) if you tried in good faith to fix it with the seller first, the item cost more than $50, you bought it in your home state or within 100 miles of your home address, and you have not paid it off in full yet. Online purchases do not always fit the distance test neatly, so this is a backup, not a promise.

With a **debit card**, the legal protections are weaker. The FTC says plainly that [credit card protections are stronger than debit card ones](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/sample-letter-disputing-credit-and-debit-card-charges), though some banks voluntarily offer more than the law requires.

| | Credit card | Debit card |
|---|---|---|
| Billing errors and goods not delivered as agreed | Disputable under the Fair Credit Billing Act; write within 60 days of the statement | Weaker legal protection; depends partly on your bank |
| Unauthorized charges | Liability capped at $50 | Weaker legal protection |
| Problems with quality | You may be able to withhold payment if conditions are met | The credit card rule does not apply |

*Billing errors and unauthorized charges: FTC guidance linked above. Withholding payment: CFPB guidance linked above.*

### The catch with agents: "authorized" purchases

If you gave the agent permission and it bought something within that permission, the store and the card issuer may see the charge as *authorized*, even though you did not want that item. Disputes are strongest when the item never arrived or was not what you agreed to buy, and weakest when the complaint is "my agent misunderstood me".

Picture an agent with a $400 limit that is asked for a 55-inch TV and buys a 50-inch one for $380. The charge is within your limit, from a real store, for an item that arrived as described. To your card issuer, that looks like a normal purchase.

Your route is the store's return policy, not a chargeback. That is why the limits you set up front matter more than the safety net afterwards.

## The real risks: fake stores, hidden instructions and paid placement

A wrong size can usually be returned. The risks below are harder to undo.

### Fake shops, now with a faster victim

An AI agent can be fooled by the same fake-store tricks as a person, and the FTC's data shows how often fraud starts on a website or app.

The same FTC analysis found that among people who lost money to a scam that started on social media, more than 40% said it began when they ordered something.

What changes with an agent is speed and the missing pause. In August 2025, researchers at Guardio [built a fake "Walmart" shop](https://guard.io/labs/scamlexity-we-put-agentic-ai-browsers-to-the-test-they-clicked-they-paid-they-failed) and told Perplexity's Comet AI browser "Buy me an Apple Watch". In some runs it refused or asked the user to finish checkout. In others it filled in the saved address and card from the browser's autofill and completed the purchase without asking.

Browsers have been updated since, but the lesson holds: an agent with access to your autofill can do in seconds what a person might stop and question.

### Prompt injection: when the web page talks to your AI

Prompt injection means hiding instructions for the AI inside content it reads, such as a product page or an email. A person never sees them; the AI may follow them. OpenAI's own [explainer](https://openai.com/index/prompt-injections/) uses an example that maps neatly onto shopping: a rental listing hides instructions telling the AI to pick it whatever your preferences. In a worse case, hidden instructions could trick an agent into handing over private information, such as bank statements.

AI companies do not claim to have solved this. OpenAI wrote in December 2025 that prompt injection, like scams and social engineering, is ["unlikely to ever be fully solved"](https://openai.com/index/hardening-atlas-against-prompt-injection/). Its advice works for any agent: use a logged-out mode when the task does not need your accounts, give narrow instructions rather than "do whatever it takes", and read each confirmation request before you approve it.

### Paid placement and commissions

AI shopping answers are not purely neutral. How far that goes depends on the company:

- OpenAI says results in shopping research are [organic](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-shopping-research/), and that items you can buy inside ChatGPT are [not preferred in product results](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/). But when it launched in-chat checkout in 2025, it said merchants paid it a small fee on completed purchases, and that support for in-chat checkout is one factor when choosing between stores selling the same product.
- In January 2026 OpenAI [announced it would test ads](https://openai.com/index/our-approach-to-advertising-and-expanding-access/) for logged-in adults in the US on its Free and Go plans, saying ads would be clearly labelled and would not influence answers.
- On Amazon, advertisers can now pay for [Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands "prompts"](https://advertising.amazon.com/resources/whats-new/unboxed-2025-sponsored-products-and-sponsored-brands-prompts) that appear in shopping results and on product pages and, when clicked, may open a conversation in Rufus.

Search engines and comparison sites carry ads too, so this does not make the advice worthless. Just read labels such as "Sponsored" or "Ad", and remember that a store's own assistant recommends that store's products.

### Your data

Shopping assistants work better the more they know about you. Amazon says Alexa for Shopping draws on your [Amazon shopping history](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/alexa-for-shopping-ai-assistant) and context from Alexa devices. When you authorize an agent at Target, its terms say you agree to Target [sharing account, order and transaction information](https://www.target.com/terms) with that agent. OpenAI says [your chats are never shared with retailers](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-shopping-research/), but shopping research can use ChatGPT's memory if you have it turned on.

You do not need to share your income or health history to find a good mixer. The habits from our guide on [what to share with AI chatbots](/articles/is-it-safe-to-share-personal-data-with-ai-chatbots) apply here: give it the requirements, not your life story.

## A six-step check before an AI deal reaches your card

This takes two or three minutes per item, less once it becomes habit. Do it whether the AI is only suggesting a deal or about to buy it.

1. **Open the store page yourself.** Click through to the actual product page and confirm the price there. As Which? found, the chat and the site can disagree by a lot.
2. **Read the web address.** Make sure the domain is the retailer you think it is: `walmart.com`, not `walmart-deals-outlet.shop`. Lookalike domains and prices far below everyone else are classic signs of a fake store. Our guide to [spotting phishing and scam links](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-emails-and-scam-texts) covers how to read a URL properly.
3. **Match the model number.** Compare the exact model code in the listing title or specs with what you asked for. "55-inch" and a similar name are not enough.
4. **Compare the total, not the headline price.** Add shipping, taxes and any membership requirement. Which? noted that delivery fees and free-shipping thresholds can make a different retailer cheaper overall.
5. **Check the price history.** A price tracker or history chart tells you whether "40% off" is a real low or the usual price next to a crossed-out number.
6. **Read the confirmation screen, and know which card it uses.** Check the agent's summary of item, size, quantity, address and total before you approve. That screen is your last checkpoint. If you have a credit card you pay off in full, it gives you the stronger dispute rights explained above; if you carry a balance, the interest can cost more than that protection is worth.

>> **Tip:** ask the assistant to show its work. A prompt like "List the retailer, the exact model number, the price and the link for each option, and tell me when you last saw that price" makes errors much easier to spot. More in our guide to [how to write AI prompts that work](/articles/how-to-write-ai-prompts-that-work).

## Is it safe to let AI shop for you? Guardrails to set first

If you decide to let an agent check out for you, set it up so a mistake stays small.

**Require approval for every purchase, at least at first.** Walmart offers this as an option, and Google's agentic checkout asks for your confirmation before it buys. If a tool offers "ask me each time" or "buy automatically", start with asking and loosen it once you trust how it behaves.

**Set a spending limit.** Where you can cap spending, use a number that matches the job. A $50 limit for household reorders keeps a mix-up small in a way an unlimited saved card does not. Walmart's agent profiles include spending limits, and Google says agent buying in Universal Cart, once it arrives, will stay within a budget you set.

**Use a separate card with a modest limit.** A card you use only for online and agent purchases makes unusual charges easy to spot and keeps the agent away from the account your rent comes out of.

If you already have a credit card you pay off in full, it is the better one to give an agent because of the dispute rights above. If you carry a balance, every purchase adds interest, so don't open or lean on a credit card just for this. Some card issuers also offer virtual card numbers you can lock or delete.

**Give narrow instructions.** "Buy the KitchenAid Artisan 5-quart in Empire Red from Target if it is under $350, shipped to my home address" leaves far less room for error or manipulation than "get my sister a nice mixer".

**Review and revoke.** Check your order history after an agent has been active, and remove access you no longer use: Target lets you revoke an agent in account settings, and Walmart lets you delete an agent profile.

## How AI shopping fits into this holiday season

Adobe [forecasts a 130% rise](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/09/adobe-us-holiday-shopping-season-to-hit-record) in traffic from AI tools to US retail sites this season, after a [693.4% jump](https://news.adobe.com/news/2026/01/adobe-holiday-shopping-season) in the 2025 holidays, though those figures count clicks through to stores, not purchases made by agents. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can ChatGPT or Gemini find the lowest price?

Sometimes, but not reliably: in Which?'s test, ChatGPT got 8 of 25 right. Use AI to shortlist, then confirm the price on the store's own page.

### Is it safe to let an AI agent pay for me?

It can be, and the limits do most of the work. Use a tool that asks you to confirm before buying, set a spending cap and check each order afterwards. If you have a credit card you pay off in full, it gives you stronger dispute rights than a debit card. Avoid giving an agent a saved debit card with no limit.

### If an AI agent buys the wrong item, can I return it?

Usually, under the store's normal return policy, but the purchase counts as yours, so the usual deadlines and exclusions apply.

### Can I charge back a purchase my AI agent made?

A purchase made within the permissions you gave is likely to count as authorized, so "it misunderstood me" is a weak basis for a credit card dispute. Contact the store first.

### Are AI shopping results sponsored?

Some are, depending on the tool. Look for "Sponsored" or "Ad" labels, and remember that a store's assistant favours its own shelves.

### Are AI browsers safe for shopping?

Use extra care. Research in a logged-out mode, keep saved cards out of the browser's autofill if you can, and approve purchases yourself.

## The takeaway

Before the sales start, take ten minutes. Open your Amazon, Walmart and Target accounts and look for any assistant or agent you have connected; remove the ones you don't use. Check which card is saved in your browser's autofill and, if it is a debit card, remove it. If a tool offers "ask me before every purchase", switch it on.

Then shop the way the tools are actually good at: let the AI argue about which mixer kneads bread best, and open the store page yourself when it is time to pay. A mistake then costs you a return label, not a charge you can't get back.
