You apply for a car loan, the dealer runs your credit, and the rate that comes back is two points higher than the one in the advert. Nobody at the desk can tell you exactly why. Somewhere in a file you have never read, a three-digit number has already decided how much the car will cost you.

That number is not a mystery, and it is not a moral verdict. It is a fairly mechanical summary of a handful of facts in your credit report, weighted in a way the main scoring company publishes openly. Once you know which facts matter and by how much, most of the folklore falls away, and the path to a better score becomes boring in the best sense: a short list of habits, a few months of patience and one or two letters if your report contains mistakes.

This guide covers the US system. It explains the difference between a report and a score, what each scoring factor actually measures, how long bad news sticks around, how to dispute errors, which popular tips are myths, and what a better score is worth in real money.

>> **In short:**
>> - Your credit **report** is the raw record kept by Equifax, Experian and TransUnion; your **score** is a number calculated from it.
>> - FICO says payment history drives about 35% of its score and amounts owed about 30%, so paying on time and keeping card balances low do most of the work.
>> - Most negative items can be reported for seven years; bankruptcies for up to ten.
>> - You can check your reports from all three bureaus free every week at AnnualCreditReport.com, and checking your own score never lowers it.
>> - Errors are common enough to be worth hunting for: in an FTC study, 26% of participants found a potential material error.

## Credit reports and credit scores are not the same thing

A credit report is a file. Each of the three nationwide credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian and TransUnion) keeps its own version, built from what lenders, card issuers and collection agencies send them. It lists your accounts, their limits and balances, whether you paid on time, any accounts sent to collections, public records such as bankruptcies, and which companies have pulled your file recently.

A credit score is a calculation run on that file at a specific moment. The most widely known family is the FICO Score, and [myFICO notes](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/credit-scores) that most credit scores use a 300 to 850 range, with higher numbers signalling lower risk to a lender. VantageScore is the main competitor, and there are dozens of FICO versions tuned for mortgages, car loans and cards.

Two consequences follow, and both confuse people constantly.

First, you do not have one credit score. You have many, and they will rarely match. The score in your banking app might be a VantageScore built on TransUnion data, while your mortgage lender pulls an older FICO version from all three bureaus. A 40-point gap between them does not mean anything is broken.

Second, the only way to change the number is to change the data underneath it. No one, including the bureaus, edits a score by hand. If the underlying data improves, every model that reads it improves too. That is why the rest of this guide spends more time on the file than on the number.

### What your score does not see

Because a FICO Score is [calculated from the information in your credit report](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/credit-scores), things that never reach the bureaus do not count. Your salary is not in your report. Neither is your savings balance, your rent history (unless a landlord or service reports it), or whether you are married. Lenders may weigh income and debts separately when they decide on an application, but the score itself is blind to them. A high earner who misses payments can have a worse score than a part-time worker who never has.

## The five factors behind a FICO Score, and what they really weigh

FICO publishes the [approximate weight of each category](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score) for the general population. The company is careful to say these are averages: the importance of each factor shifts depending on what is in a particular file, and someone with a thin history is scored differently from someone with twenty years of accounts.

The chart makes the priority obvious. Two categories, payment history and amounts owed, account for roughly two thirds of the score. Credit mix and new credit together are worth about as much as the length of your history. If you only have the energy for two habits, pick the first two.

| Factor | Approx. weight | What it looks at | What actually helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment history | 35% | Whether past bills were paid on time; how late, how recent and how many problems there were | Never missing a due date; autopay for at least the minimum |
| Amounts owed | 30% | Balances relative to limits, especially on cards; how much is left on loans | Low reported card balances; paying before the statement closes |
| Length of credit history | 15% | Age of your oldest and newest accounts and the average age | Keeping old no-fee cards open; not opening many new accounts at once |
| New credit | 10% | Recently opened accounts and hard inquiries | Spacing applications; rate-shopping within a short window |
| Credit mix | 10% | The blend of revolving credit (cards) and instalment loans | Nothing, usually; never borrow just to improve the mix |

### Payment history: the factor that forgives slowly

This is the heaviest category, and it works asymmetrically. Years of on-time payments build up quietly; one serious delinquency shows up loudly. myFICO is explicit that a few late payments are not an automatic "score-killer", and that how recent and how severe they were matters. A single 30-day late payment from four years ago weighs far less than a 90-day late payment from last spring.

The practical lesson is to make a missed payment impossible rather than unlikely. Set autopay for the minimum on every card and loan, then pay the rest manually. The minimum protects your record; the manual payment protects you from interest.

### Amounts owed: where fast gains live

Amounts owed is the factor most people can move within a single month. For credit cards, scoring models look at utilisation: the share of your available revolving credit that your reported balances use. Someone with a $10,000 total limit and $6,000 of reported balances is at 60%; the same person with $600 reported is at 6%.

There is no penalty memory here the way there is for late payments. Utilisation is generally read from whatever balances are on your report now, which is why paying down cards is the fastest lever available. It is also why the timing of your payment matters, which the tip below explains.

![Two credit cards resting on a laptop keyboard, ready for an online payment](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8937438/pexels-photo-8937438.jpeg)
*Card balances are usually reported once a month, around the statement date. Photo: Leeloo The First / [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/close-up-shot-of-credit-cards-on-laptop-keyboard-8937438/)*

>> **Tip: pay before the statement, not just before the due date.**
>> Card issuers typically send your balance to the bureaus around the date your statement is generated, which can be weeks before the payment is due. If you charge $2,500 a month and pay in full on the due date, your report may still show $2,500 every month. Paying most of it a few days before the statement closes means a much smaller number is reported, with no change to what you spend.

### Length of history, new credit and mix

The remaining 35% rewards patience and restraint more than any action. Length of history improves simply by waiting, and by not closing your oldest accounts without a good reason. New credit covers hard inquiries and recently opened accounts. myFICO says its scores [only consider inquiries from the last 12 months](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score), although they stay on your report for two years, and that for many people one extra inquiry [takes less than five points off](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/credit-reports/credit-checks-and-inquiries).

Credit mix is the most overrated factor on the internet. FICO itself says you do not need one of each type of account and that mix is unlikely to be decisive on its own. Taking out a loan you do not need, and paying interest on it, to nudge a 10% category is a poor trade.

## What moves a score up, and what knocks it down

A useful way to think about scoring is to sort events by how big and how lasting their effect is.

**Big, lasting damage** comes from serious delinquencies: payments 60 or 90 days late, accounts charged off or sent to collections, foreclosures, repossessions and bankruptcies. These sit in the payment-history category and fade gradually rather than disappearing all at once.

**Big, temporary swings** come from utilisation. Max out a card for a holiday and your score may drop sharply; pay it off and the next report can undo most of the damage. This is why people see their score jump around by 20 or 30 points from month to month without having done anything wrong.

**Small, temporary dips** come from hard inquiries and new accounts. They are real but modest, and they shrink as the account ages.

**Slow, steady gains** come from time: each month of on-time payments, each year your accounts get older.

What does not move a FICO Score at all is checking it yourself. myFICO states that [checking your own credit through a soft inquiry will not hurt your score](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/credit-reports/credit-checks-and-inquiries). Pre-approval offers and many account reviews by existing lenders are also soft inquiries.

### Shopping for a loan without being punished for it

Rate shopping for a mortgage, a car loan or a student loan creates several hard inquiries in a short period, and scoring models are built to expect that. According to [myFICO](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/credit-reports/credit-checks-and-inquiries), FICO Scores ignore these inquiries for the 30 days before scoring. After that, older versions of the formula treat inquiries of the same type within any 14-day span as a single inquiry, and newer versions use a 45-day span. Since you may not know which version a lender uses, the safe move is to do all your comparison shopping within two weeks.

Credit card applications get no such grouping. Five card applications in a month are five inquiries.

## How long negative items stay on your report

Federal law caps how long most bad news can be reported. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that bureaus can generally [report most negative information for seven years](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-my-credit-report-en-323/), and bankruptcies for up to ten.

| Item on your report | How long it can be reported | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Late or missed payments | Up to 7 years | [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-my-credit-report-en-323/) |
| Most other negative information (collections, charge-offs) | Generally 7 years | [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-my-credit-report-en-323/) |
| Lawsuits and judgments | 7 years or until the statute of limitations runs out, whichever is longer | [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-my-credit-report-en-323/) |
| Bankruptcy | Up to 10 years | [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-my-credit-report-en-323/) |
| Hard inquiries | 2 years on the report; FICO counts only the last 12 months | [myFICO](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/whats-in-your-credit-score) |

Two caveats are worth knowing. The CFPB notes that these limits do not apply when a report is pulled for a job paying more than $75,000 a year or for an application for more than $150,000 of credit or life insurance. And "can be reported for seven years" is not the same as "hurts equally for seven years". A collection from six years ago, surrounded by clean history, matters much less to a score than it did in its first year.

That second point is the most encouraging fact in this guide. You cannot erase accurate negative information early, but you can bury it under fresh, positive data, and the model will notice.

## Check your reports first: where errors hide

Before any improvement plan, read the source data. The Federal Trade Commission confirms that the three nationwide bureaus let you [check your credit report from each of them once a week for free](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/free-credit-reports) at AnnualCreditReport.com, the only site authorised for this purpose. You can also order by phone at 1-877-322-8228. The free reports do not include a score, which is fine: the report is what you need to read.

Errors are not rare. The most thorough public study remains the FTC's congressionally mandated review of credit report accuracy, in which 1,001 consumers went through 2,968 reports line by line with the help of a study associate. Its findings, summarised in the FTC's [2013 statement to Congress](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/public_statements/prepared-statement-federal-trade-commission-discussing-most-recent-commission-report-congress-under/130507fcra.pdf), are the source for the chart below.

### What to look for, line by line

Go through each report with a pen and your own records:

- **Identity details.** Misspelled names, addresses you never lived at, a Social Security number with a digit wrong. Mixed files, where another person's data lands in yours, often start here.
- **Accounts you do not recognise.** These can be honest mistakes or signs of identity theft.
- **Late payments you know were on time.** Bank statements are your evidence.
- **Wrong balances or limits.** A card showing a $1,000 limit when it is really $5,000 inflates your utilisation.
- **Duplicates.** The same debt listed twice, for instance by the original lender and by a collector, both showing a balance.
- **Stale items.** Negative entries older than the reporting limits in the table above.
- **Closed accounts shown as open**, or accounts you closed shown as "closed by grantor".

## How to dispute an error on your credit report

![Paper collage of a hand holding a magnifying glass over a bill pulled from an envelope](https://images.pexels.com/photos/5849566/pexels-photo-5849566.jpeg)
*A dispute succeeds on evidence, so gather statements and letters before you write. Photo: Monstera Production / [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/cutout-paper-of-man-examining-bills-through-magnifying-glass-5849566/)*

The FTC's guide to [disputing errors on your credit report](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/disputing-errors-your-credit-reports) sets out the process, and it is more effective than many people expect.

1. **Dispute with every bureau that shows the error.** Each bureau keeps its own file, so a correction at Experian does nothing at Equifax.
2. **Also dispute with the business that supplied the information**, such as the card issuer or collector.
3. **Put it in writing.** Explain what is wrong and why, identify each item, and ask for it to be corrected or deleted. Online dispute forms work, but keep screenshots and confirmation numbers.
4. **Send copies, never originals,** of supporting documents: bank statements, payoff letters, a police or identity-theft report.
5. **Keep a dated record** of everything you sent and received.

The bureau generally has 30 days to investigate, and it must forward your evidence to the business that reported the item. If that business finds the information is wrong, it must notify all three nationwide bureaus. You receive the results in writing, and if your report changes you get a free copy that does not count against your free reports. You can also ask the bureau to send notice of the correction to anyone who received your report in the past six months, or to an employer who pulled it in the past two years.

If the investigation does not go your way, you can ask for a brief statement of the dispute to be added to your file. You can also complain to the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/), which forwards complaints to companies and publishes how they respond.

>> **Common mistake: paying someone to do what the law lets you do free.**
>> Credit repair companies cannot remove accurate, current negative information, and they have no special access to the bureaus. Everything a legitimate service can do, from requesting reports to filing disputes, you can do yourself at no cost. Be especially wary of anyone who asks for money up front or tells you to dispute information you know is correct.

## A realistic plan to improve your credit score

Most "boost your score fast" advice either overpromises or quietly assumes you have spare cash to throw at debt. The plan below is ordered by impact and by how soon results show. It assumes you are starting from an average or damaged file, not from nothing.

![A person on a bed sorting payment slips next to an open laptop showing an online account](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6862449/pexels-photo-6862449.jpeg)
*Steps one to three take an evening at a laptop with your statements. Photo: cottonbro studio / [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/overhead-shot-of-a-person-holding-a-piece-of-paper-6862449/)*

1. **Pull all three reports this week.** Mark every error and every negative item with its date. You now know what you are dealing with.
2. **Dispute the errors.** Start with anything that looks like fraud or a mixed file, then wrong late payments and wrong limits. Results usually arrive within about a month.
3. **Set autopay for the minimum on every account.** This single step stops new damage, which matters more than anything else you will do.
4. **Bring any account that is currently past due up to date.** An account that is still delinquent keeps generating fresh negative marks each month it stays behind.
5. **Lower your reported card balances.** Pay down the highest-utilisation cards first, and start paying before the statement date. This is the step most likely to show up on your next report.
6. **Ask for a higher limit on cards you already have**, but only if you are confident you will not spend into it. Before you agree, ask your issuer whether it will run a hard inquiry.
7. **Leave old accounts alone.** Keep long-standing no-fee cards open and use them lightly now and then so the issuer does not close them for inactivity.
8. **Pause new applications for six to twelve months** unless you genuinely need the credit.
9. **If your file is thin, add positive data deliberately.** A secured card or becoming an authorised user on a relative's long-standing, well-managed card can give the model something to score. Both carry risks: an authorised user can be hurt if the primary cardholder misses payments.
10. **Recheck every few months.** Pull your reports, confirm corrections stuck and look for anything new.

### What to expect, honestly

Steps two and five can move a score within one or two reporting cycles, because errors and high balances are read from the current file. Steps three, seven and eight work on a scale of months and years, because they rely on history building up. Serious delinquencies fade over several years; there is no shortcut, and anyone who sells one is selling a story.

If you are dealing with collections, be cautious about paying an old debt without reading the terms first. Ask the collector to confirm in writing how the account will be reported once paid, and keep that letter.

## Credit score myths that refuse to die

**"Checking my score will lower it."** Not your own check. Soft inquiries, including viewing your own report or score, do not affect FICO Scores.

**"Carrying a small balance on a card helps my score."** Paying interest does nothing for your score. What scoring models see is the balance reported, not whether you paid in full afterwards. Pay in full every month and you get the same credit history without the interest bill.

**"Closing old cards cleans up my credit."** Often the opposite. Closing a card removes its limit from your utilisation calculation and, over time, can shorten your average account age.

**"My income affects my credit score."** It does not, because income is not in your credit report. It matters to lenders when they assess an application, which is a separate decision.

**"Shopping around for a mortgage will wreck my score."** Not if you do it in a short window. Multiple mortgage, auto or student loan inquiries close together are treated as one for scoring.

**"Paying off a debt removes it from my report."** Paying changes the status to paid, but the history of late payments stays for the full reporting period. The account stops getting worse; it does not vanish.

**"There is one official credit score."** There are many models and many versions, built on three separate files. The number you see in an app is a useful guide, not the one every lender sees.

>> Two buyers borrow $300,000 over 30 years. One, with a stronger credit profile, is offered 6.5%; the other is offered 7.5%. The monthly principal-and-interest payment is about $1,896 versus $2,098, a gap of roughly $202 a month. This is the honest case for caring about your score. It is not a grade on your character. It is a price signal, and in a big loan the signal is worth thousands of dollars.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast can I raise my credit score?

It depends on what is holding it down. If the problem is high card balances or an error on your report, a score can recover within one or two monthly reporting cycles once balances fall or the error is corrected. If the problem is recent late payments or a collection, improvement is gradual and takes many months of clean history.

### What is a good credit score?

FICO Scores generally run from 300 to 850. There is no single legal cut-off for "good", and each lender sets its own tiers. Higher is always better, but beyond a certain point many lenders offer their best pricing and extra points make little practical difference.

### Does a hard inquiry hurt my score for two years?

The inquiry stays on your report for two years, but FICO Scores only consider inquiries from the last 12 months. For many people, a single inquiry costs fewer than five points.

### Can I remove a late payment that was accurate?

Not through a dispute: accurate information can be reported for up to seven years. Some people write to the lender asking for a goodwill adjustment after a one-off slip with an otherwise clean record. Lenders are under no obligation to agree, so treat it as a polite request, not a strategy.

### Do I need to pay to see my credit report?

No. You can get free reports from Equifax, Experian and TransUnion every week at AnnualCreditReport.com. Many banks and card issuers also show a free score in their apps.

## The takeaway

Start this week by pulling your three free reports and reading them properly. Dispute anything wrong, switch on autopay for every minimum, and change when you pay your cards so that lower balances get reported. Those three moves handle most of what a score measures.

After that, the work is mainly waiting: no rush of new applications, old accounts kept open, every bill paid on time. Credit scores reward dull consistency, which is good news, because dull consistency is something anyone can do.
