Every month the news reports an inflation figure, and every month plenty of people look at it and think: that can't be right. Their rent went up more than that. Their weekly shop definitely went up more than that.

Often, both things are true. The official number isn't fake, and your experience isn't imagined. They're measuring different things.

## What the official number measures

Inflation figures like the Consumer Price Index (CPI) track the price of a "basket" of goods and services that represents what an average household buys: food, energy, housing costs, transport, clothes, entertainment and so on. Each item gets a weight based on how much households spend on it overall.

The headline rate is the change in the price of that whole basket compared with the same month a year earlier.

Two words in that explanation do a lot of work: **average** and **year**.

## Reason 1: you aren't the average household

Nobody actually spends money in exactly the proportions of the official basket. If you rent in an expensive city, housing is a much bigger share of your spending than average. If you drive a lot, fuel matters more. If you're on a tight budget, food and energy take up more of your income than they do for a typical household.

When the things you buy most rise faster than the overall basket, your personal inflation rate is genuinely higher than the headline. Some statistics agencies publish calculators that let you put in your own spending pattern; the results can differ a lot from the national figure.

## Reason 2: lower inflation doesn't mean lower prices

This is the big one. When inflation falls from, say, 8% to 3%, it means prices are rising *more slowly*. It doesn't mean they've gone back down.

Imagine a loaf of bread that cost 1.00 two years ago, 1.08 last year and 1.11 this year. The inflation rate on bread has dropped sharply. But you're still paying 11% more than you were two years ago, and that's what your memory compares against.

Prices generally ratchet upwards. Falling inflation stops the climb from getting steeper; it doesn't reverse it. What makes higher prices bearable over time is wages catching up, and that tends to happen slowly and unevenly.

## Reason 3: we notice some prices much more than others

Psychologically, not all prices are equal. You see the price of milk, coffee and petrol constantly. You buy a television or a laptop once every few years.

Frequently bought items shape our sense of inflation far more than their share of the official basket would suggest. If groceries are up sharply but electronics are flat or falling, the official index averages them out — your gut doesn't.

## Reason 4: some costs sit outside the basket

What counts and how it's counted varies by country, and it's not always obvious. Mortgage interest payments, for example, are treated differently across measures: some indexes exclude them or capture housing costs in other ways. If your mortgage rate jumped when your fixed deal ended, that shock can be much larger than anything the headline figure shows.

Similarly, "shrinkflation" — the same price for a smaller pack — is something statistics agencies try to adjust for, but it adds to the feeling that you're getting less for your money.

## Reason 5: quality adjustments

When a product changes, statisticians try to separate a genuine price rise from a change in what you're getting. If a new phone costs more but is meaningfully better, part of the price rise may be treated as paying for quality rather than inflation.

That's reasonable in principle. In practice, if you didn't want the extra features and couldn't buy the old model, it still feels like paying more for the same thing.

## So which number should you use?

Both, for different jobs.

The official figure is the right tool for understanding the economy as a whole, comparing across years and countries, and following what central banks are likely to do with interest rates.

For your own budget, your personal inflation rate is what matters. A simple way to estimate it:

1. Look at your bank statements from this month and the same month last year.
2. Group spending into a few categories: housing, food, energy, transport, everything else.
3. Note which categories went up because prices rose, versus because you bought more.

It's rough, but it'll tell you more about your own situation than any national average can.

## The takeaway

When the news says inflation is "under control" and your bills say otherwise, nobody is necessarily lying. The headline is an average of price *changes*; you live with price *levels*, in your own particular mix. Understanding the gap won't make the shopping cheaper, but it does make the numbers a lot less infuriating.
