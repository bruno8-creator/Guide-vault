The word turns up in a headline, then in a group chat, then in a conversation at work about whether the hiring freeze is "temporary". Suddenly you are wondering whether to stop contributing to your retirement account, whether your job is safe, and whether you should be paying off the credit card faster or hoarding cash instead.

Most of what gets said about recessions in those moments is either wrong or useless. The famous "two negative quarters" rule is not how the United States officially defines one. The official call usually arrives months after the recession has started, sometimes after it has already ended. And the damage to a household rarely comes from the recession itself: it comes from losing an income while carrying expensive debt and no cash buffer.

This guide explains how US recessions are actually defined and dated, which indicators are worth your attention (and what they can and cannot tell you), what has historically happened to jobs, prices, interest rates and markets, and a concrete preparation plan you can start this month, whatever the headlines say.

>> **In short:**
>> - In the US, recessions are dated by a committee at the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), not by a two-quarter GDP rule.
>> - The NBER looks for a significant, widespread decline that lasts more than a few months, and often announces it long after the fact.
>> - The yield curve and the Sahm rule are useful warning lights, not crystal balls.
>> - For households, the main risk is job loss, and unemployment usually keeps rising after a recession has officially ended.
>> - The best preparation is boring: a bigger cash buffer, less high-interest debt, a clear view of your fixed costs and a refreshed CV.

## What a recession actually is (and why "two quarters" is not the rule)

Ask most people for the definition of a recession and they will say "two consecutive quarters of falling GDP". It is a handy rule of thumb used in many countries and by many journalists. It is not the definition used in the United States.

The US has no government agency that declares recessions. That job falls, by long convention, to the Business Cycle Dating Committee of the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/research/business-cycle-dating), a private, non-profit research organisation. According to the committee's own [FAQ](https://www.nber.org/business-cycle-dating-procedure-frequently-asked-questions), a recession is "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and that lasts more than a few months."

That sentence contains three tests, which economists shorthand as depth, diffusion and duration. The decline has to be large enough to matter, broad enough that it is not confined to one industry or region, and long-lasting enough not to be a blip. The NBER says each of the three criteria needs to be met "to some degree", although an extreme reading on one can partly make up for a weaker reading on another. The 2020 recession, which lasted just two months, qualified because the collapse was so deep and so widespread.

### Why GDP alone is not enough

The NBER is explicit that it does not use the two-quarter test. Its FAQ notes that most recessions it has identified do include two or more quarters of falling real GDP, "but not all of them". The 2001 recession is the example it gives of one that did not.

Instead, the committee watches a set of monthly measures. It names real personal income less transfers, nonfarm payroll employment and real personal consumption expenditures, and also looks at employment from the household survey and industrial production. There is no fixed formula for weighting them.

The reverse case matters too. In 2022, the Bureau of Economic Analysis initially reported that real GDP fell in both the first and second quarters (estimates at the time put the declines at 1.6% and 0.6% at annual rates, per [BEA's third estimate for the second quarter](https://www.bea.gov/news/2022/gross-domestic-product-third-estimate-gdp-industry-and-corporate-profits-revised-2nd)). By the rule of thumb, that was a recession. Yet payrolls were growing strongly and unemployment was low, and the NBER never declared one. If you had sold everything or panicked about your job on the strength of the GDP headline alone, you would have been reacting to a definition that the official arbiter does not use.

### Why the official call always comes late

The second thing to understand is timing. The NBER deliberately waits until it is sure. In its words, the committee "waits long enough to avoid any doubt about the existence of a peak or trough." Its FAQ says announcements have come as early as four months after a peak (for the February 2020 peak) and as late as 21 months after a trough (for the March 1991 trough).

In practice, that means the recession you are worrying about may already be under way, or already over, before it is official. For your household, the official label is close to irrelevant. What matters is what is happening to hiring, to your employer and to your own income.

## The warning signs people watch, and how much to trust them

No single indicator reliably calls recessions in advance. A handful have decent track records, and it is worth knowing what they actually measure so you can read the headlines with a calmer eye.

### The yield curve

The yield curve compares interest rates on government bonds of different maturities. Normally, lending money to the government for ten years pays more than lending it for three months, so the curve slopes upward. When short-term rates rise above long-term ones, the curve is said to be "inverted".

An inversion tends to happen when the central bank has pushed short rates high to fight inflation and investors expect it to cut rates later because growth will weaken. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York tracks this relationship and publishes its own recession-probability estimate based on the slope of the curve; you can see the current reading on its [yield curve page](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/capital_markets/ycfaq).

The honest caveat: the lead time between an inversion and a downturn has varied widely, and an inversion that began in 2022 lasted a long stretch without the quick recession many forecasters expected. A signal that has worked well in the past is not a guarantee, especially when the economy has unusual features. Treat an inversion as a reason to check your own preparation, not as a countdown.

### The Sahm rule

Named after economist Claudia Sahm, this rule watches unemployment rather than markets. As described on the [Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis FRED page](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SAHMREALTIME), it signals the start of a recession when the three-month moving average of the national unemployment rate rises by 0.50 percentage points or more relative to its lowest three-month average over the previous 12 months.

The appeal is that it is simple and uses data that come out every month. Its weakness is the flip side: it is triggered by unemployment that is already rising, so it tells you a downturn has probably begun, not that one is coming. It can also be set off by things other than layoffs, such as a fast increase in the number of people looking for work.

### The data the NBER itself watches

If you want to look at what the referees look at, follow the monthly jobs report from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (payroll employment and the unemployment rate), consumer spending and personal income from the BEA, and industrial production from the Federal Reserve. A recession shows up as several of these falling together for months, not one bad print.

| Indicator | What it measures | Where to find it | What it is good for | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yield curve (long-term minus short-term rates) | Bond market expectations for rates and growth | New York Fed | Early warning, often a year or more ahead | Has given long, uncertain lead times |
| Sahm rule | Rise in the 3-month average unemployment rate | FRED (St. Louis Fed) | Confirming a downturn quickly | Signals after unemployment is already rising |
| Nonfarm payrolls | Number of jobs in the economy | BLS monthly jobs report | Direct read on hiring | Large revisions after first release |
| Real consumer spending | What households buy, adjusted for inflation | BEA | Shows whether demand is cracking | Noisy month to month |
| Real GDP | Total output, quarterly | BEA | Big picture | Quarterly, late and revised; not the official test |

>> **Common mistake:** Treating one indicator as a verdict. An inverted yield curve, a weak GDP quarter or a single bad jobs report is a reason to look more closely, not to sell your investments or stop paying into your pension. The official arbiter weighs several measures over months, and so should you.

## What has historically happened to jobs

For most households, a recession is a job-market event. Businesses facing falling sales cut hiring first, then hours, then staff. People who keep their jobs feel it through smaller raises, fewer bonuses and less bargaining power. People who lose their jobs find it takes longer to land the next one.

Now line those peaks up against the NBER dates, and a pattern appears that matters more than any single number. In several recessions, unemployment kept climbing well after the economy had officially started to recover:

- The 1990–91 recession ended in March 1991, but [unemployment](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/UNRATE) did not peak until June 1992, at 7.8%.
- The 2001 recession ended in November 2001, but unemployment peaked in June 2003, at 6.3%.
- The Great Recession ended in June 2009, but unemployment peaked in October 2009, at 10.0%.

The 2020 recession was the exception. Unemployment jumped to 14.8% in April 2020, the same month the NBER marks as the trough, and then fell quickly as lockdowns lifted.

The practical lesson is that "the recession is over" on the news does not mean the job market has healed. If you lose work near the end of a downturn, you may be job hunting during the period when unemployment is at its worst. Plan your cash buffer around that, not around the official dates.

### Who tends to be hit hardest

Historically, job losses have been concentrated in cyclical sectors: manufacturing, construction, and industries that depend on discretionary spending such as travel, hospitality and retail. Newer and younger workers, people on temporary contracts and those paid partly through commission or overtime tend to see their income fall first. Healthcare, utilities and government employment have usually been steadier, though not immune.

That is not a reason to change careers in a panic. It is a reason to be honest about where your own job sits on that spectrum, because it should shape how big your safety net needs to be.

## What happens to prices, interest rates and markets

### Prices

The usual pattern is that inflation slows during and after a recession. Weaker demand makes it harder for businesses to push through price rises, and some prices, such as fuel, often fall sharply. That is cold comfort if your income has fallen, but it does mean groceries and petrol stop rising as fast.

There are exceptions. The recessions of the 1970s were set off partly by oil price shocks, and high inflation and rising unemployment arrived together, a combination that came to be called stagflation. A recession caused by a supply shock does not automatically bring relief at the till.

### Interest rates

The Federal Reserve normally responds to a recession by cutting its policy rate to encourage borrowing and spending. In the two most recent recessions, it took its target range all the way down to 0 to 0.25 percent: in [December 2008](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20081216b.htm) during the financial crisis, and in [March 2020](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20200315a.htm) at the start of the pandemic.

For households, that cuts both ways. Variable-rate debt, such as some credit lines and adjustable-rate mortgages, can become cheaper, and it may become a good time to refinance if your credit and income are solid. Savers, on the other hand, see the rates on savings accounts and certificates of deposit fall. If you are earning an attractive rate on cash at the time of writing, do not assume it will last through a downturn. Our guide to [how Fed interest rate changes affect your debt and savings](/articles/how-fed-interest-rate-changes-affect-you) shows which accounts follow a cut within days and which barely move.

One catch: banks tend to tighten lending standards in a recession. Cheaper rates on paper do not help if you cannot qualify. The best time to secure a credit line or refinance is usually while your job and credit score are in good shape, not after a layoff.

### Stock markets

Stock prices tend to fall around recessions, sometimes sharply, because investors anticipate lower profits. But markets look forward. They often fall before a recession is recognised and frequently begin to recover before it has ended, which is exactly when the news feels worst.

That timing gap is why trying to "get out before the recession" is so hard to get right. You need to be right twice: when to sell and when to buy back. Missing the rebound can cost more than sitting through the fall. For long-term money such as retirement savings, the usual principle is that your investment mix should reflect when you will need the money and how much loss you can tolerate, decided calmly in advance, rather than headlines. Money you will need within the next few years arguably should not be in stocks at all, recession or not.

## A household recession plan, step by step

![Financial documents, a calculator and cash spread across a table during household budget planning](https://images.pexels.com/photos/5849563/pexels-photo-5849563.jpeg)
*Preparation starts with knowing your fixed costs and what you owe. Photo: Monstera Production / [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/person-signing-loan-agreement-for-purchase-of-apartment-5849563/)*

None of the steps below depends on predicting the timing of a recession. They make your household sturdier whether one arrives next year or in five.

1. **Work out your "essentials-only" monthly number.** Add up only what you would have to keep paying if your income stopped: rent or mortgage, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments, food, transport, childcare and medicine. Leave out subscriptions, eating out and savings contributions. This number, not your current spending, is what your emergency fund must cover.
2. **Build cash towards several months of that number.** A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses. Given that job searches stretch out after recessions, households with one income, variable pay or a job in a cyclical industry have good reason to aim for the upper end or beyond. Keep it in an insured, easy-access savings account, not in stocks.
3. **Attack high-interest debt.** Credit card balances at high rates are the most dangerous thing to carry into a downturn: the interest keeps compounding while your income may not. Paying them down also frees up monthly cash flow, which is exactly what you need if you lose income.
4. **Avoid locking in new fixed costs.** A recession scare is a poor moment to take on a larger car loan, a new lease or a bigger mortgage that only works if your income stays exactly where it is.
5. **Check your safety nets now.** Find out how unemployment insurance works in your state and roughly what it would pay, what your employer offers in severance, and what would happen to your health insurance if you lost your job. Doing this research while calm is far easier than during a layoff.
6. **Make yourself easy to rehire.** Update your CV and your professional profiles, note recent achievements with numbers, and keep in touch with contacts. If your field offers certifications that improve your options, the quiet period before a downturn is a good time to get them.
7. **Leave your long-term investments alone unless your plan changes.** If your retirement mix was right for your age and goals last year, a recession forecast is not by itself a reason to change it. If you realise you were taking more risk than you could stomach, adjust it deliberately, not in a panic.
8. **Write down your "if I lose my job" plan.** Which expenses go first, which bills you would call to ask for hardship options, how long your cash lasts. A written plan turns a crisis into a checklist.

>> **Worked example:** Suppose your household's essentials-only costs come to $3,200 a month. Three months of cover is $9,600; six months is $19,200. If you currently have $4,000 in savings and $5,000 on a credit card, a sensible order is to keep the $4,000 as a starter buffer, put spare cash towards the card until it is cleared, then build savings towards the $9,600 mark and beyond. Illustrative figures only.

![Hands counting dollar bills next to a calculator on a table](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6266283/pexels-photo-6266283.jpeg)
*An emergency fund sized to your essential costs is the single biggest buffer against a recession. Photo: Tima Miroshnichenko / [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/person-holding-dollar-bills-while-using-a-calculator-6266283/)*

### If you are already worried about your job

Some steps become more urgent if your employer is cutting back or your industry is shedding jobs. Pause non-essential spending now rather than after the news arrives. Use any employer benefits you are entitled to while you still have them, such as healthcare appointments or training budgets. And if you have the choice, think twice before taking a hardship withdrawal from a retirement account: taxes and penalties can make it an expensive source of cash, so it is usually a last resort.

> A recession is not something you can avoid. Being forced to sell investments, borrow at high rates or take any job at any pay because you ran out of cash is something you can usually avoid.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are we in a recession right now?

Only the NBER can make that call for the US, and it does so with a delay. The NBER publishes its announcements on its [business cycle dating page](https://www.nber.org/research/business-cycle-dating). To judge for yourself at the time of writing, look at the monthly jobs report, the unemployment rate and consumer spending. If several of them are weakening together for months, the economy is probably in or near a downturn.

### Is a recession the same as two quarters of negative GDP?

No, not in the US. The two-quarter rule is a common shorthand, but the NBER defines a recession as a significant, widespread decline in activity lasting more than a few months, and it looks mainly at monthly indicators such as employment, income and spending. The 2001 recession did not include two consecutive quarters of falling GDP, and the two negative quarters initially reported in 2022 were not declared a recession.

### How long do recessions usually last?

Since 1945, US recessions have lasted between 2 and 18 months, according to NBER dates. Most were under a year. The effects on jobs usually last longer, because unemployment has often kept rising for months after the official end.

### Should I sell my stocks before a recession?

Nobody can reliably time it, and markets often fall before a recession is recognised and recover before it ends. Selling means you also need to decide when to buy back, and missing a rebound can be costly. A more reliable approach is to keep money you need in the next few years out of stocks, and to make sure your long-term mix reflects your timeline and tolerance for losses.

### How much emergency savings do I need for a recession?

A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses, meaning only what you would have to pay if your income stopped. Households with a single earner, irregular pay or a job in an industry that tends to cut staff in downturns have good reasons to aim higher.

### Do prices go down in a recession?

Inflation usually slows, and some prices, such as fuel, can fall. But overall prices rarely drop much, and recessions caused by supply shocks, such as those of the 1970s, have combined rising unemployment with high inflation.

## The takeaway

A recession is a label applied, after the fact, by a committee that weighs a range of data. It is worth understanding so you can ignore the noise around it: the two-quarter rule, the breathless forecasts and the urge to rearrange your finances every time the yield curve makes the news.

What deserves your attention is your own exposure. Work out your essentials-only monthly number, build cash towards several months of it, clear expensive debt, and keep your skills and contacts current. Do that and the next recession, whenever it comes, becomes something to get through rather than something that knocks your household over.
