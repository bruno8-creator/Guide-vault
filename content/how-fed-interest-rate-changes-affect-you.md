The headline says the Fed "held rates steady", or "cut", or "raised", and for a day or so it's the top business story everywhere. Then nothing seems to happen. Your mortgage payment is the same, and so is your credit card statement. Meanwhile the savings account that paid a decent rate last month is paying a bit less, or a bit more, and nobody told you why.

That gap between the headline and your own accounts is what this guide is about: how Fed rate changes affect you, account by account. The Federal Reserve sets one very short-term interest rate that ordinary people never pay directly. Some of your money is wired to that rate and moves within days; some isn't connected at all. Knowing which is which tells you when to act and, just as usefully, when to ignore the news.

>> **In short:** the Fed's rate reaches your variable debt fast, your savings slowly, and your fixed loans not at all.
>> - The Fed sets a target range for the federal funds rate, an overnight rate between banks. Banks then move the prime rate, usually the next day.
>> - Anything variable and tied to prime (most credit cards, HELOCs) follows within a billing cycle or two.
>> - Fixed-rate mortgages track longer-term bond yields, not the Fed, and can move the opposite way.
>> - A quarter-point change is worth a few dollars a year on a typical card balance. Your own rate, not the Fed's, is what moves the needle.

## Where things stand right now

On October 28, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee [kept the federal funds target range at 3.75% to 4.00%](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm). The vote was 10 to 2, and the two dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, wanted to *raise* rates by a quarter point, with the statement still describing inflation as elevated. The committee's next scheduled meeting is [December 8–9, 2026](https://federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm).

So the current situation is a pause with a hawkish tilt: no change for now, and at least some officials more worried about inflation than about growth. If you're wondering why the Fed won't try to push prices back to where they were, our guide to [why prices stay high even when inflation slows](/articles/why-prices-stay-high-when-inflation-slows) explains. Treat these figures as a snapshot. Everything below works the same whether the next move is up, down or nowhere.

## How Fed interest rate changes reach your accounts

### Step 1: the federal funds rate

The federal funds rate is, in the Fed's own words, the rate "that banks [pay for overnight borrowing](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/money_12856.htm) in the federal funds market." You will never borrow at it. Its job is to act as the anchor for the rest of the system: when it moves, the cost of short-term money for banks moves, and they pass that on.

The decision is made by the FOMC, which has [12 voting members](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm): the seven Fed governors, the president of the New York Fed, and four of the other eleven regional bank presidents on a one-year rotation. It holds eight scheduled meetings a year, roughly every six weeks. The Fed itself is candid that the effects of its decisions on the wider economy are ["not direct or immediate."](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/money_12856.htm) Your accounts are a different matter: some of them react almost at once.

### Step 2: the prime rate

The prime rate is the base rate that large US banks publish for lending. The Fed's H.15 data, republished on FRED, takes it from [what most of the country's 25 largest insured banks are charging](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DPRIME). It isn't set by the Fed, but in practice banks have kept it three percentage points above the top of the Fed's target range, moving it in step with every Fed decision of recent years, up or down. You can see that in the official numbers:

- After the Fed's half-point cut on [September 18, 2024](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/monetary20240918a1.pdf) (to 4.75–5.00%), prime moved from 8.50% to 8.00% [the next day](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/prime).
- After the [December 18, 2024 cut](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20241218a.htm) (to 4.25–4.50%), prime dropped to 7.50%.
- After the Fed [raised the range by a quarter point on September 16, 2026](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm) (to 3.75–4.00%), prime went back up from 6.75% to 7.00% the next day.
- Today, with the top of the range still at 4.00%, prime stands at [7.00%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DPRIME) (latest reading, October 27, 2026).

**Prime moves within a day or two of a Fed decision.** That's the fastest link in the whole chain.

### Step 3: variable-rate products

Many consumer loans are priced as an index plus a margin. The index moves with the market; the margin is a fixed number of points the lender adds. On credit cards, the CFPB names the [U.S. prime rate as a typical index](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/when-can-my-credit-card-company-increase-my-interest-rate-what-can-i-do-to-get-the-rate-back-down-en-69/), and its research on card pricing treats prime as [the reference point issuers build APRs on](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/examining-the-factors-driving-high-credit-card-interest-rates/). Home equity lines of credit work the same way, usually with [prime or a Treasury rate as the index](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf).

So when prime moves a quarter point, a card priced at "prime + 15" moves a quarter point too. Your margin doesn't change.

### Step 4: everything else

Fixed-rate loans you already have don't change at all. New fixed-rate loans, including 30-year mortgages, are priced off longer-term rates that markets set every day, based on where they expect inflation, growth and the Fed to be over many years. The Fed influences those expectations but doesn't control them, which is the part most people get wrong.

## Which of your accounts the Fed actually touches

This is the table to keep. "When" means how soon a change in the Fed's rate usually shows up, assuming the product is linked at all.

| Product | Fixed or variable? | Does a Fed move affect it? | When? | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Credit card | Usually variable, tied to prime | Yes, by the same amount as prime | Soon after prime moves, on your issuer's schedule | Pay it down; the Fed's moves are tiny next to the APR itself |
| HELOC | Usually variable (prime or Treasury index) | Yes | At the next rate adjustment set in your agreement | Check your index, margin and caps; consider fixing part of the balance if offered |
| Existing fixed-rate mortgage | Fixed | No | Never, unless you refinance | Nothing, unless rates fall enough to justify refinancing |
| New fixed-rate mortgage | Fixed once you lock | Indirectly, through bond markets | Unpredictable; can move before, after or against the Fed | Watch mortgage rates, not the Fed; shop several lenders |
| Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) | Fixed for an initial period, then variable | Yes, after the fixed period, via its index | At each scheduled reset | Know your reset date, index, margin and caps |
| Auto loan | Almost always fixed | Only for new loans | When you take out the loan | Get preapproved before visiting a dealer |
| Federal student loans | Fixed for the life of the loan (if first disbursed since July 2006) | No (for existing loans) | Never | Nothing to do on rates |
| Private student loans | Fixed or variable | Yes if variable | At the lender's adjustment dates | Check your terms; consider refinancing variable debt carefully |
| Savings and money market accounts | Variable, at the bank's discretion | Usually, but banks choose how much | Weeks to months, unevenly | Compare your rate against other insured banks |
| CDs | Rate locked for the term | Only for new CDs | When you open or renew | Lock a longer term if you expect cuts and won't need the money |

The pattern is clear enough. **Only variable-rate debts follow the Fed closely, and savings follow it loosely.** Most of a typical household's big-ticket borrowing (the mortgage, the car, federal student debt) is fixed and doesn't move once you've signed.

## The math: what a quarter point is worth in dollars

The Fed usually moves in steps of 0.25 percentage points. Say you carry a $5,000 card balance and keep $20,000 in savings. Here is what one of those steps does to each.

**The card.** The average rate on credit card accounts that are charged interest was [22.36% in August 2026](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TERMCBCCINTNS), according to Federal Reserve data. At that rate, $5,000 carried for a year costs roughly $1,118 in interest (a simplified calculation that ignores compounding and payments). A quarter-point cut takes the APR to 22.11% and saves you about **$12.50 a year**, around a dollar a month. A hike costs you the same.

**The savings.** If your savings rate moves by the same quarter point, the difference is **$50 a year**: four times the card effect, simply because the balance is bigger. That assumes your bank passes the full change on, which it may not.

Now set those against the moves you control:

| Change | Yearly effect | Compared with a quarter-point Fed move |
|---|---|---|
| Fed cuts 0.25 points, $5,000 card | About $12.50 saved | — |
| Fed moves 0.25 points, $20,000 savings | About $50 | — |
| You pay off the $5,000 card | About $1,118 saved | Roughly 90 times the card effect |
| You move $20,000 from 0.5% to 3.5% (illustrative rates, not a quote) | About $600 earned | Twelve times the savings effect |

For most households, **the Fed's decision is a rounding error next to the rate you're personally paying or earning**. A 22% card is expensive whether the Fed is at 4% or 3%, and a savings account paying almost nothing is a bad deal in any rate environment.

Mortgages are the exception, because the balances are large. On a $300,000, 30-year fixed loan, the monthly principal-and-interest payment is about $1,996 at 7.00% and about $1,946 at 6.75%: roughly $50 a month, or $600 a year, for a quarter point. (Illustrative figures from the standard loan formula, excluding taxes and insurance.) The catch is that mortgage rates don't reliably move when the Fed does.

## Mortgages: why fixed rates don't follow the Fed

This is where people make the most expensive timing mistakes.

A 30-year fixed mortgage is long-term money. Lenders and the investors who buy mortgage bonds price it against long-term rates, mostly the 10-year Treasury yield. Researchers at the Atlanta Fed found that over the past 20 years, mortgage rates have tracked [the 10-year yield more closely than the fed funds rate](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/2025/11/10/not-joined-at-the-hip-relationship-between-the-fed-funds-rate-and-mortgage-rates), partly because the average mortgage only lasts about seven to ten years before it's paid off or refinanced. Long-term yields respond to inflation expectations, growth expectations, government borrowing and investor appetite, as well as to what markets think the Fed will do over the coming years.

That means mortgage rates often move *before* the Fed acts, once a cut or hike is expected. And sometimes they go the other way entirely.

The clearest recent example came in late 2024. The Fed cut by half a point in September, then again in November and December, a full percentage point in total. Over roughly the same months, the average 30-year fixed rate went *up*, according to Freddie Mac's weekly survey:

The Atlanta Fed's analysis puts numbers on the mismatch: between September 2024 and January 2025, the fed funds rate fell by about 80 basis points while [the 10-year Treasury yield rose about 90](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/2025/11/10/not-joined-at-the-hip-relationship-between-the-fed-funds-rate-and-mortgage-rates). Mortgages followed the bond market, not the Fed.

Today's numbers show the same gap. The Fed's range tops out at 4.00%, yet the 30-year fixed averaged [7.18% in the week to November 5, 2026](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/mortgage30us), with the [10-year Treasury at 4.84%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/dGs10) the same day.

>> **Watch out:** Waiting for "the next Fed cut" before locking a mortgage rate. By the time a widely expected cut happens, it's usually already priced into mortgage rates. Watch actual mortgage quotes, and lock when the payment works for your budget.

### Adjustable-rate mortgages are different

An ARM does respond to rate changes, just not right away. The CFPB explains that an ARM usually has [an introductory rate for a set period](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-a-fixed-rate-and-adjustable-rate-mortgage-arm-loan-en-100/), after which it resets at regular intervals to an index plus a margin, subject to any caps. The margin is [fixed when you take the loan](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/for-an-adjustable-rate-mortgage-arm-what-are-the-index-and-margin-and-how-do-they-work-en-1949/) and doesn't change.

So a hike today matters to you only if your index is still higher at your next reset date. If you have an ARM, find your reset date, margin and caps in the loan papers; those tell you far more than the Fed's statement.

## Credit cards and HELOCs: the fast lane

These are the products where a Fed move shows up first, because their index, usually prime, tends to reset the day after the announcement.

On a credit card, a variable rate going up because its index went up is one of the cases where the issuer can raise the rate on your *existing* balance, as the CFPB explains in its guide to [when card rates can increase](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/when-can-my-credit-card-company-increase-my-interest-rate-what-can-i-do-to-get-the-rate-back-down-en-69/). That's different from an issuer simply deciding to charge you more, which generally requires 45 days' notice and applies to new purchases. In practice, an index-driven change [typically appears within one or two billing cycles](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/how-do-fed-rate-cuts-impact-credit-cards), though timing varies by issuer; your cardholder agreement says exactly when.

![A hand tapping a credit card on a payment terminal for a contactless payment](https://images.pexels.com/photos/11316617/pexels-photo-11316617.jpeg)
*On most cards, the balance you carry is charged at a variable APR tied to prime. Photo: Towfiqu barbhuiya / [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/photo/a-person-tapping-a-card-on-a-payment-terminal-11316617/)*

What the Fed doesn't control is the margin, and that's where most of a card's cost sits. The CFPB found that the gap between the prime rate and average card APRs [hit an all-time high in 2021](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/examining-the-factors-driving-high-credit-card-interest-rates/), even while defaults were at record lows. With prime at 7.00% and the average assessed card rate above 22%, more than two-thirds of a typical APR is margin above prime, which no Fed decision touches.

A HELOC is priced the same way but on much larger balances, so it's the variable debt where Fed moves matter most. The CFPB notes that your payment can rise or fall [without you drawing a single extra dollar](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf), and that some lenders allow a slice of the balance to be switched to a fixed rate, usually at a higher but predictable rate. And when the draw period ends and repayment begins, monthly payments are often [much higher](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/), rate changes or not.

If your card rate is what's hurting, a better credit score will do far more for you than the Fed. Our guide on [how credit scores work and how to improve yours](/articles/how-credit-scores-work-and-how-to-improve-yours) covers the steps.

## Car loans and student loans: mostly fixed

According to the CFPB, [most auto loans have fixed rates](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-i-negotiate-the-interest-rate-on-an-auto-loan-with-the-dealer-en-795/), so a Fed move only matters if you're about to buy, and even then it's rarely the biggest factor. The same CFPB page warns that dealers often add a markup to the rate the lender actually approved, and that the rate is negotiable. Getting preapproved by a bank or credit union before you walk into the showroom will usually save you more than a quarter-point Fed cut ever could. Shopping several lenders within a short window (the CFPB cites roughly 14 to 45 days) generally counts as a single credit inquiry.

Federal student loans first disbursed since July 2006 carry [a fixed rate for the life of the loan](https://edfinancial.studentaid.gov/interest-rates-for-federal-student-loans). The rate is set each July 1 for new loans; for 2026–27 it's 6.52% for undergraduate Direct Loans, 8.07% for graduate unsubsidized loans and 9.07% for PLUS loans. Once you have the loan, the Fed is irrelevant to it.

[Private student loans](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-are-private-student-loans-en-2136/) are another matter: they may have variable rates, which can climb when rates rise. If you have one, check whether it's variable and what index it uses. Be cautious about refinancing *federal* loans into private ones to chase a lower rate: the move is permanent, and it [strips away protections that only federal loans carry](https://navyfederal.org/makingcents/college-planning/refinancing-student-loans-pros-and-cons.html), from repayment plans pegged to your income to the possibility of having the debt forgiven.

## Savings, money market accounts and CDs

Savings rates are where the Fed's moves are most noticeable for people without much debt, and also where banks have the most freedom.

A savings or money market account rate is variable and set at the bank's discretion. When the Fed raises rates, some banks pass the increase on quickly to compete for deposits; others barely move, counting on customers who never switch. When the Fed cuts, expect your rate to drift down. Nothing forces a bank to match the Fed in either direction.

So the most valuable thing you can do is check what you're actually being paid and compare it with other insured banks and credit unions. The gap between a bank's standard savings rate and a competitive one can dwarf any Fed move. Deposits at an FDIC-insured bank, or an NCUA-insured credit union, are covered [up to $250,000](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-certificate-of-deposit-cd-en-917/), so moving to a less familiar but insured institution doesn't add risk within that limit.

A CD works differently: you agree to leave the money for a set term, and in exchange the rate you get when you open it is normally locked until it matures. Taking money out early [means paying a penalty](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-certificate-of-deposit-cd-en-917/).

### Should you lock in a CD?

If you expect rates to fall, locking in today's rate on a CD protects that income. If you expect them to rise, a shorter term keeps you flexible. Nobody, including the Fed, knows the path for sure, so a sensible middle ground is to split the money across several terms (say 6, 12 and 24 months) so that some of it renews regularly.

One rule matters more than any rate forecast: **only put money in a CD that you're confident you won't need before it matures.** Your emergency fund belongs in an easy-access account, as we explain in [how to build an emergency fund](/articles/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund). Money for goals five or more years away usually belongs in investments rather than cash; see [index funds explained for beginners](/articles/index-funds-explained-for-beginners).

## Should you refinance when rates fall?

If you already have a fixed-rate mortgage, refinancing is the only way a lower rate reaches you. Whether it's worth doing comes down to one number: how many months it takes for the saving to pay back the cost.

Say a lender quotes you a refinance that costs $6,000 and cuts your payment by $100 a month (illustrative numbers). Divide one by the other and you need 60 months, five years, to come out ahead. **If you're likely to sell before then, it's a bad deal however good the new rate looks.**

To get the cost side right, use the Loan Estimate each lender gives you: it shows the total cost of the new loan and any prepayment penalty, so you can compare offers like for like. Don't underestimate that cost, either; the CFPB's refinancing handout reminds borrowers that a refinance involves [many of the same steps and costs as your original mortgage](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_should_i_refinance_handout.pdf).

Then look at the term, because a lower payment can hide a higher total. Restarting a 30-year clock on a loan you've been paying for eight years stretches your debt back out, so you can end up paying more interest overall. The CFPB suggests asking about a custom term, such as 22 years, to match what you have left.

A few situations make a refinance hard to justify even when the rate looks tempting, and the CFPB lists them: your current loan carries a prepayment penalty, your credit score has dropped since you borrowed, your home has lost value, or you expect to move within a few years.

## What to do when rates go up, down or stay put

The details shift with each scenario, but look at how much of the advice stays the same.

| | Fed raises | Fed cuts | Fed holds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit cards and other variable debt | Pay them down; their cost rises before anything else's | Slightly cheaper, not enough to change your payoff plan; keep paying | Nothing changes automatically; a good moment to clear the card |
| HELOC or ARM near a reset | Check your caps and ask whether a fixed-rate option is available | Your payment may ease at the next adjustment | Review the terms while nothing is moving |
| Savings | Move to a competitive insured account so you actually collect the higher rate | Expect the rate to drift lower; a CD can lock in today's rate for cash you won't need | Check your rate against other insured banks |
| Buying a home or car, or refinancing | Budget at today's quotes and get preapproved | Watch mortgage rates, not the Fed, and run the break-even numbers | Set a target payment and act when quotes reach it |

Two things don't fit neatly in a table. When rates fall, don't let cheaper borrowing talk you into a bigger loan than your budget can carry. And if you've been waiting for a cut to buy or refinance, a pause is a reminder that tying your plans to a meeting date rarely works; a payment you can afford is a better trigger.

>> **Tip:** Write down three numbers once a year: your credit card APR, your savings account rate, and your mortgage or HELOC rate (and the reset date if it's variable). That takes ten minutes and tells you more about what Fed decisions mean for you than any amount of news coverage.

Rates are only one way the wider economy reaches your wallet. If you want the bigger picture, see [what a recession means for your money](/articles/what-a-recession-means-for-your-money), which also covers how the Fed tends to respond to downturns.

## Frequently asked questions

### How does a Fed rate cut affect me?

Mostly through variable-rate debt and savings: credit cards and HELOCs get a little cheaper soon after, while savings rates drift lower over the following weeks or months. Fixed-rate loans you already hold, from the mortgage to the car loan, stay exactly as they are.

### Do mortgage rates follow the Fed?

Not directly. Fixed mortgage rates are priced off long-term bond yields, mainly the 10-year Treasury. After the Fed cut by a full point in late 2024, the average 30-year rate rose from about 6.1% to 7.0% within four months.

### Should I wait for rates to fall before buying a house?

Trying to time the Fed is unreliable, because mortgage rates often move ahead of it or in the opposite direction. Decide what monthly payment you can afford and buy when price and rate together fit that number. If rates fall a lot later, refinancing may be an option, provided the break-even math works.

## The takeaway

A quarter point is rarely what decides whether your finances are in good shape. A 22% card balance, a savings account paying close to nothing, or a variable loan you don't understand will cost you far more than any single Fed meeting.

So before the next one on December 8–9, write down the three numbers from the tip above. If one of those three numbers looks bad, that's your project for the month, whatever the Fed decides. Fix it, and the next rate announcement becomes something you read about rather than something you have to react to.
