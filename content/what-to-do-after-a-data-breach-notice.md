The envelope looks like junk mail, or the email lands between a shipping update and a newsletter. "Notice of Data Security Incident." Somewhere in the second paragraph is the line that matters: *your information may have been involved.* After that comes careful legal language, an offer of free credit monitoring and an enrollment code.

Most people either panic and sign up for everything, or shrug and throw it away because it is the third one this year. Neither is right. A data breach notice is a set of instructions in disguise: the part that lists *what* was exposed tells you exactly what to fix. A leaked email address calls for very different action than a leaked Social Security number.

This month shows the range. On November 9, TechCrunch [reported](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3186559) that a password left inside Wyndham's Android app opened a database holding customer names, email addresses, member numbers and login tokens; Wyndham would not say whether it would notify customers. Earlier in the month, Malwarebytes [described](https://malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/11/applebees-customer-data-exposed-by-password-left-in-app) a similar flaw at Applebee's that exposed names, dates of birth, phone numbers and ZIP codes, but not card numbers or passwords. Below: how to check a notice is genuine, what to do on day one, and what each type of leaked data requires.

>> **In short:** the list of what leaked decides what you do, and most fixes take minutes.
>> - Verify the notice without using its links or phone numbers: go to the company's own website, or look it up in a state attorney general's public breach list.
>> - Find the "what information was involved" section. That list decides your next steps.
>> - Passwords and cards are fixed in minutes. A Social Security number calls for a credit freeze at all three bureaus, which is free and does not affect your score.
>> - Free credit monitoring is worth accepting, but it only tells you after something has happened. A freeze stops it from happening.
>> - Expect follow-up scams that use the breach as bait, including fake "compensation" and settlement messages.

## First, make sure the notice is real

Fake breach notices exist for an obvious reason: "your data was stolen, click here to protect yourself" is a perfect phishing lure. So the first rule is the same one we give in our guide to [spotting phishing emails and scam texts](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-emails-and-scam-texts): do not use the contact route the message gives you. No links, no QR codes and no phone number from the letter itself, at least until you have confirmed the notice through a route you found yourself. The FTC gives the same advice for emails claiming your data is for sale on the dark web: [contact the company through a website or number you already know is real](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2022/09/did-you-get-email-saying-your-personal-info-sale-dark-web).

Three checks, about ten minutes in total.

**Go to the company's website by typing the address yourself.** Companies often post a notice or FAQ about the incident on their own site. If the letter names a monitoring provider, that page should mention the same one.

**Look the breach up in a state attorney general's database.** Few guides mention this step, yet it is one of the most reliable, because some states publish the breach letters companies file with them. California's attorney general runs a [searchable list of breach notices](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/databreach/list); any business that has to notify more than 500 California residents from a single breach must submit a sample of its letter, according to the office's [reporting rules](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/databreach/reporting).

Washington's attorney general publishes a [breach notification directory](https://www.atg.wa.gov/data-breach-notifications) showing the date reported, the number of Washingtonians affected, the types of information compromised and a PDF of the actual notice. You do not need to live in either state to use them. If a national company wrote to you, a copy may well sit in one of these lists, ready to compare with yours.

**Check what the letter asks you to do.** A real notice explains what happened and offers help. A request to reply with your full Social Security number or card number "to confirm your identity", a fee, or a threat to close your account today are all red flags.

![A printed letter lying on top of the brown envelope it arrived in](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1627618998627-70a92a874cc2)
*Whether it comes by post or email, only one section of the notice really decides what you do. Photo: Mediamodifier / [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/photos/ehI8qokwP7s)*

>> **Tip:** read the letter for one section only. Many notices have a heading such as "What information was involved"; California's breach law [requires that exact heading](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_civ_code_section_1798.82) in notices sent to its residents. Write down every item on that list. The rest of the letter matters much less.

One caveat: a breach can be real even if you cannot find it in any state list. Not every state publishes notices, and small breaches may fall below the filing threshold.

## The first 24 hours: a short checklist

This order works for most people, with the cheap, fast steps first.

1. **Confirm the notice is real** using the checks above.
2. **List what was exposed** from the letter.
3. **Change the password** for that account if any login data was involved, plus every other account where you reused it. Turn on two-step verification while you are there.
4. **Call your bank or card issuer** if a card or account number was exposed, using the number on the back of your card.
5. **Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion** if your Social Security number was exposed. If you are not sure, freezing anyway costs nothing.
6. **Decide on the free monitoring** the company offers, after reading the terms (more on that below).
7. **Tell the people you live with** to expect scam messages that mention the company by name.

Steps 3 to 5 usually take under an hour in total. The rest can wait until the weekend.

## What to do, based on what was leaked

The FTC's [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/#/Info-Lost-or-Stolen) organizes its breach advice by data type for a reason: what a criminal can do with your email address is not what they can do with your bank details. The table below draws on IdentityTheft.gov's [printable data breach checklist](https://www.identitytheft.gov/assets/pdf/Data_Breach.pdf) and the FTC's current advice on passwords, cards, credit freezes and medical records.

| What was exposed | What a criminal can do with it | What you do | How urgent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email address only | Target you with convincing phishing | Expect scam emails that mention the company; check the address on Have I Been Pwned | Low |
| Email and password (even hashed) | Log in to that account, and to any other account using the same password | Change the password, and change it everywhere you reused it; turn on two-step verification | Same day |
| Login tokens or session data | Stay signed in as you without your password | Sign out of all sessions in the account settings, then change the password | Same day |
| Credit card number | Make purchases | Call the issuer, cancel and replace the card; update automatic payments; watch statements | Same day |
| Debit card number | Drain your checking account | Same as a credit card, and do it faster: your protection depends on how quickly you report | Same day |
| Bank account and routing number | Withdraw or move money, set up fraudulent payments | Call the bank; ask whether to close the account and open a new one; watch transactions | Same day |
| Social Security number | Open credit, file a fake tax return, claim benefits | Freeze credit at all three bureaus; get an IRS IP PIN; check credit reports | Within days |
| Driver's license number | Pose as you with fake ID, open accounts | Consider calling your state DMV; check credit reports; consider a freeze | Within days |
| Medical or health insurance data | Get care or prescriptions billed to you | Read every Explanation of Benefits; request your records if something looks wrong | Ongoing |
| Date of birth, phone, home address | Make scams more convincing; help answer security questions | Expect targeted scams; tighten security questions and account PINs where you can | Low to medium |

To see how this works in practice, say you were one of the Applebee's customers. Your list would be name, date of birth, phone number and ZIP code: the last row. There is no card to cancel and no password to change. Your job is to be suspicious of any call or text that knows your birthday and mentions the restaurant.

A few rows need more than a table cell.

### Passwords: reuse is the real problem

If passwords were exposed, assume they can be cracked, even if the company says they were "hashed". The FTC's post-breach advice starts here: [change the password for that account and for any other account where you used the same or a similar one](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2022/10/have-you-been-affected-data-breach-read), and turn on multi-factor authentication.

**Start with your email account.** Whoever controls your inbox can reset the passwords on almost everything else. A password manager is the long-term fix, because it makes reuse unnecessary. If login tokens leaked too, as in the Wyndham case, use "sign out of all devices" to end any copied session.

### Cards and bank accounts: speed decides who pays

Federal law treats credit and debit cards differently, which is why the table says "faster" for debit. According to the FTC's page on [lost or stolen cards](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/lost-or-stolen-credit-atm-and-debit-cards), if only your credit card *number* is misused while the card is still in your wallet, you are not responsible for charges you did not authorize.

A stolen debit card number is covered too, but only on a clock: flag the fraudulent transactions no later than 60 calendar days after the bank sends your statement, or your possible losses grow. There is a practical difference as well. Debit fraud takes money out of your own checking account, and you are without it while the bank investigates.

The FTC checklist recommends cancelling the card, getting a new number and updating automatic payments. For bank account details, it suggests closing the account and opening a new one.

### Driver's license and medical data

For a driver's license, consider calling your state DMV, but do not count on a new number: [Oregon's DMV](https://www.oregon.gov/odot/DMV/Pages/Data_Breach.aspx) changes it only with proof of fraud. Then check your credit reports.

Medical identity theft is quieter. The FTC's guide to [medical identity theft](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-medical-identity-theft) lists the warning signs: a bill or Explanation of Benefits for care you did not receive, a collector chasing medical debt you do not owe, or an insurer saying you have hit a benefit limit. The Explanation of Benefits, the statement your insurer sends after each claim, is your best early warning. If something looks wrong, ask the provider for your records and report errors in writing; the FTC says the provider must respond within 30 days.

## Credit freeze vs fraud alert: which one you need

These two tools get mixed up constantly. Both are free by federal law: since September 2018, the [Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2018/09/starting-today-new-federal-law-allows-consumers-place-free-credit-freezes-yearlong-fraud-alerts) has made credit freezes free nationwide and stretched initial fraud alerts from 90 days to a year.

| | Credit freeze | Fraud alert |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Blocks most lenders from seeing your credit report, so new credit is usually not approved | Tells lenders to verify your identity, usually by calling you, before opening an account |
| How strong | Strong: no report, no new account | Weaker: relies on the lender following the alert |
| How long | Until you lift it | One year (renewable); seven years for an extended alert if you are an identity theft victim |
| Cost | Free to place, lift and reinstate | Free |
| How to set it up | Contact each bureau separately | Contact one bureau; it must tell the other two |
| Effect on your credit score | None | None |
| What you have to do before borrowing | Lift it temporarily at the bureau the lender uses | Nothing, but expect a verification call |

Sources: FTC, [Is a credit freeze or fraud alert right for you?](https://consumer.ftc.gov/credit-freeze-or-fraud-alert-right-you) and [Credit Freezes and Fraud Alerts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts).

My view is simple: **if your Social Security number was in the breach, freeze.** A fraud alert is a polite request; a freeze is a hard block. The FTC describes a freeze as especially useful [after a data breach](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/09/get-credit-freeze-stop-identity-thieves).

It does not stop you using the cards and accounts you already have. When a new lender, landlord or insurer needs to pull your credit report, you lift the freeze for a few days, online or by phone, and it goes back on afterwards. It also has no effect on your score; our guide to [how credit scores work](/articles/how-credit-scores-work-and-how-to-improve-yours) explains what does.

A fraud alert fits a narrower case: you expect to apply for credit often in the coming months and do not want to lift freezes each time.

### How to freeze your credit at all three bureaus

You have to do this three times, because a freeze at one bureau does not apply to the others. Online is fastest. By law, a bureau has to place a freeze within one business day of an online or phone request, and lift one within an hour; mail requests can take three business days, according to the [FTC's summary of the law](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2018/09/starting-today-new-federal-law-allows-consumers-place-free-credit-freezes-yearlong-fraud-alerts).

| Bureau | Online | Phone | Temporary lift |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equifax | [Security freeze page](https://www.equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-freeze/) (myEquifax account) | (888) 298-0045 | Online through myEquifax, or by phone |
| Experian | [Freeze center](https://www.experian.com/help/credit-freeze/) (free Experian account) | 1-888-397-3742 | Online "thaw" with a start and end date |
| TransUnion | [Credit freeze](https://www.transunion.com/credit-freeze) (TransUnion Service Center) | 800-916-8800 | Online with dates, up to 15 days ahead |

Details from each bureau's own pages (TransUnion's phone number is in its [freeze FAQ](https://www.transunion.com/credit-freeze/credit-freeze-faq)), at the time of writing.

![A hand with a pen ticking off items on a handwritten checklist in a notebook](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1768055105012-4c53791b2877)
*Three bureaus, three separate requests: tick each one off as you go so none is missed. Photo: Jakub Żerdzicki / [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/photos/fXlL5I0IvK0)*

You will create an account at each bureau, and the sign-up pages also promote paid products such as monitoring or a "credit lock". You do not need them; the freeze itself is free. Save the three logins in your password manager. The hard part is not placing a freeze, it is finding the login two years later when you want a car loan.

When you do borrow, the FTC suggests asking the lender which bureau it will check and lifting the freeze only there. Experian [suggests lifting all three](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/how-to-temporarily-lift-a-security-freeze/), since some lenders check more than one, so ask the lender. Either way, a scheduled thaw that ends on its own beats removing the freeze and forgetting to put it back.

Children can be frozen too. Parents can freeze the credit of a child under 16 for free; the process is different from an adult freeze, and each bureau's instructions are linked from the FTC's [freeze page](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts).

## If your Social Security number was exposed

This case deserves the most attention, and it is common. In Washington's latest annual breach report, released on November 9, 2026, Social Security numbers were exposed in 148 of the 197 breaches reported to the state, [over 75% of the total](https://www.atg.wa.gov/news/news-releases/ag-s-annual-data-breach-report-recommends-stronger-protections-washingtonians). Those breaches generated more than 3.5 million notices to Washington residents alone.

You cannot easily change an SSN, so the goal is to make it hard to use.

**Freeze your credit** at all three bureaus, as above, so no one can open new cards or loans in your name.

**Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN.** With an [IP PIN](https://www.irs.gov/identity-theft-fraud-scams/get-an-identity-protection-pin) on your account, a federal return filed under your SSN is only accepted if it carries that six-digit code, which nobody but you and the IRS has. You qualify if you have an SSN or ITIN and can prove who you are, and the code changes every year.

>> **Watch out:** the IRS says its online IP PIN tool is offline for maintenance from November 16, 2026 until mid-January 2027. Set a reminder for its return; request before you file.

Form 15227 covers people who cannot verify online and whose adjusted gross income was under $84,000 ($168,000 for married couples filing jointly); the PIN arrives by mail in about four to six weeks. An in-person appointment is the fallback. The IRS has not said whether the outage affects these routes, so set the reminder either way.

**Do not file an identity theft affidavit just because of the breach.** Form 14039 is for people who believe someone has already used their SSN for taxes. The IRS says on its [affidavit guidance](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/when-to-file-an-identity-theft-affidavit) that the warning signs include an e-filed return rejected because one was already filed under your SSN, IRS notices about wages from an employer you never worked for, or a tax transcript you never requested. Without one of those, the IP PIN is the right tool.

**Check your credit reports.** You can get a free report from each bureau every week at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/10/you-now-have-permanent-access-free-weekly-credit-reports), the site the FTC directs people to; it warns that look-alike sites may charge you or steal your details. Look for accounts, addresses or inquiries you do not recognize.

The chart below shows why the credit freeze comes first. Of the identity theft reports the FTC received in 2024, credit card fraud was by far the most common type, and loans or leases came third.

## Is the free credit monitoring worth it?

Many breach letters offer free credit monitoring, and forums argue about it endlessly. The honest answer: take it if it is truly free, but understand what it is.

Credit monitoring watches your credit report and alerts you to certain changes, such as a new loan or card. That is useful, but it is a smoke alarm, not a lock: it tells you *after* someone has opened an account in your name. A credit freeze stops the account being opened in the first place. **If you only have the energy for one, choose the freeze.**

The FTC is clear about the gaps. Its guidance on [identity theft protection services](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft) says credit monitoring will not alert you to withdrawals from your bank account or to someone filing a tax return with your SSN. Most identity monitoring will not catch tax refund fraud or benefits claimed in your name either. And the insurance bundled with these plans typically covers what it costs you to clean up, such as fees and time off work, rather than the money a thief actually took.

Still, the FTC's [video on what to do after a data breach](https://consumer.ftc.gov/media/79862) recommends using free services the breached company offers, such as credit monitoring. I agree, with three conditions:

- **Enroll through a route you have verified.** Use the enrollment page named on the company's own breach notice or in the attorney general's copy, not a link from an email you cannot trace.
- **Read what happens when it ends.** Before you hand over a card number, check whether the "free" period rolls into a paid subscription. If the sign-up asks for payment details for a free service, ask why.
- **Do not let it replace the freeze.** It is easy to sign up, feel protected and never get round to freezing. Do both.

A common mistake is paying for an identity protection plan after a breach. The FTC lists free alternatives for the core of what those plans do: a freeze at the three bureaus, your own free credit reports, a one-year fraud alert and a recovery plan from IdentityTheft.gov if something goes wrong.

## Watch for the follow-up scams

A breach in the news is a gift to scammers. Within days, messages start arriving that name the breached company.

Some dangle money (a refund, a gift card, "compensation"); others threaten to shut your account unless you confirm your details. Malwarebytes warned Applebee's customers about both kinds. Because the scammer may know your real name, phone number or birthday from the breach itself, these messages can be unusually convincing.

The fix is the one from the start of this article: check any offer by going to the company's website yourself, never through the message. Our [phishing guide](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-emails-and-scam-texts) covers the other warning signs and what to do if you already clicked.

A newer twist: scammers also pose as the regulators. The FTC published an alert on November 12, 2026 about [fake FTC websites](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/11/how-spot-fake-ftc-websites-and-ftc-impersonators), noting that real FTC sites end in ".gov" and that it has demanded the removal of more than 30 impostor sites since its impersonation rule took effect in April 2024.

And if you paste your letter into an AI chatbot to decode it, remove the enrollment code and account numbers first; [here is why](/articles/is-it-safe-to-share-personal-data-with-ai-chatbots).

## Settlements and class actions: should you file a claim?

Large breaches often lead to a class action and, eventually, a settlement. You may get an email or postcard saying you are a class member and can claim cash, monitoring or reimbursement for costs the breach caused you. Claims have deadlines, so do not sit on it.

If you are eligible and the claim is genuine, filing is usually worth the few minutes, but keep expectations modest: a fixed fund is shared among many claimants. In an FTC staff [study of consumer class action settlements](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/consumers-class-actions-retrospective-analysis-settlement-campaigns/class_action_fairness_report.pdf) (2019), the median payment across the cases studied was $69.

The risk is fake claim sites. When the Equifax settlement opened in 2019, the FTC warned that scammers might build sites that [look like the official claims website](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2019/07/equifax-data-breach-beware-fake-settlement-websites).

Its advice carries over to any settlement: get to the claim site through an official source, such as a regulator's announcement or the notice itself once verified, not through a search ad or a link someone sends you. Never pay to file, because filing a claim is free. And treat unsolicited calls urging you to file as a likely scam.

One thing that looks suspicious but is not: claims are typically processed by a private class action administrator, as the same FTC study describes, so the official claim site may not end in ".gov". What is not normal is a site that asks for your full SSN and bank login, or charges a "processing fee".

## Check whether your email was in other data breaches

[Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/) shows which other leaks you have been in, for free: type your email address and it lists the known breaches that included it. At the time of writing it covers more than 1,000 breached websites and billions of accounts.

Two things to know from its [FAQ](https://haveibeenpwned.com/FAQs). A match is a historical record: changing your password fixes the risk but will not remove the entry. And no match does not prove you were never breached, because the service holds only a subset of all leaked data.

The "Notify Me" option is worth setting up: you verify your address once and get an email if it turns up in a newly added breach. The same site runs Pwned Passwords, which checks whether a password has appeared in a breach. If one of yours has, stop using it everywhere.

## If someone already used your data

An account you did not open, a charge you did not make or an unexplained tax rejection means the breach has turned into identity theft.

Report it at [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/), or by phone at 877-438-4338, as the FTC explains in its [identity theft guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-identity-theft). The site builds a personal recovery plan based on what happened, tracks your progress and generates pre-filled letters to send to credit bureaus, businesses and debt collectors.

An FTC identity theft report also qualifies you for an [extended fraud alert](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts) that lasts seven years and takes you off prescreened credit and insurance offers for five. Keep a log of every call, with dates and names.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I freeze my credit after a data breach if my SSN wasn't exposed?

It is not essential if only an email, password or card number leaked; fix those directly. But a freeze is free, does not affect your score and blocks new accounts in your name, so there is little downside to freezing anyway, especially if you are not planning to borrow soon.

### Does a credit freeze stop all identity theft?

No. It blocks new credit accounts in your name. It does not stop someone using a card number you already have, filing a tax return with your SSN or claiming government benefits. That is why the IRS IP PIN and checking your statements still matter.

### Do I need to do anything if the breach was years ago?

Yes, if you never acted on it. Old breaches still feed password-guessing attacks and phishing. The FTC specifically warns against [ignoring notices about old accounts](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2022/10/have-you-been-affected-data-breach-read), because people reuse passwords. It also pays to keep the devices you sign in from patched; here is how to check whether [your device is still getting security updates](/articles/is-your-device-still-getting-security-updates).

## The takeaway

Take the letter out again tonight and find the list of what was exposed. If it shows only an email, a password or a card number, you can be finished before dinner.

If your Social Security number is on it, open the three bureau sites and place the freezes now, then put a reminder in your calendar for mid-January 2027, when the IRS IP PIN tool comes back. The monitoring enrollment and any settlement claim can wait for the weekend. Neither one stops a new account; the freeze does.
