Somebody at work mentions that "the government is going to put $1,000 into your retirement account" starting next year. It sounds like a rumour, or an ad. It is neither. From the 2027 tax year, a federal program called the Saver's Match will add money directly to the retirement accounts of lower- and moderate-income workers who save for themselves. The first payments go out in 2028, after you file your 2027 return.

If you are 18 or older, not a full-time student, not claimed as someone's dependent, and your income is below a set limit, the Treasury will match 50% of up to $2,000 you put into a workplace plan or IRA in 2027. That is up to $1,000 per person, or up to $2,000 for a couple where both spouses save.

The headline is easy. The details are where people will trip up: which income figure counts, how the match shrinks, which accounts can take the money and what an early withdrawal costs you. Below, each one is worked through with numbers, followed by a dated checklist and an honest list of what is still undecided.

>> **In short:** a 50% federal match on retirement savings for lower- and moderate-income workers, paid into an account rather than as a refund.
>> - The Saver's Match starts with **2027 contributions**. You claim it on your 2027 return, filed in 2028, using a new **Form 8880-A**.
>> - The match is **50% of up to $2,000** you contribute, so **up to $1,000 per person**.
>> - Full match up to **$20,500** of modified AGI for single filers, **$30,750** for heads of household and **$41,000** for married couples filing jointly. It shrinks above those levels and reaches zero at **$35,500**, **$53,250** and **$71,000**.
>> - The money cannot go into a Roth account directly. It goes to a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), the TSP or a traditional IRA whose provider accepts it.
>> - It is not taxed when deposited, but it is taxed when you take it out, and early withdrawals can trigger extra tax.

## How the Saver's Match works

The Saver's Match comes from section 103 of the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022, which added a new section 6433 to the tax code. The [statute](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/html/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleF-chap65-subchapB-sec6433.htm) applies to tax years beginning after December 31, 2026, and it does something unusual: the "credit" it creates is paid by the Treasury as a contribution into a retirement account, rather than being knocked off your tax bill.

The mechanics, as the [IRS describes them](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match), are simple. During 2027 you contribute to an eligible retirement plan or IRA (there is no minimum). When you file your 2027 return in 2028, you attach Form 8880-A to claim the match and say where to send it. The Treasury then deposits the match into a retirement account in your name.

According to the IRS, there is no action to take in 2026. Contributions made this year do not count toward the match, though they still count for the existing Saver's Credit on your 2026 return.

On timing, the IRS [news release announcing Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/node/163436) says payments begin in 2028 for 2027 contributions. [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-48.pdf) itself says the match is payable "as soon as practicable" after you file a return that claims it. Nobody has published a target number of days or weeks yet.

There is one small exception to "it goes into an account". If your calculated match is more than zero but under $100, the statute lets you choose to take it as an ordinary refundable tax credit instead. That avoids a $30 deposit landing in an account you have to keep open just to receive it.

## Who qualifies for the Saver's Match

You need to clear five tests for the tax year in question. According to the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match), you must:

- be at least 18 by the end of the year;
- not be a full-time student, as the tax code defines it (broadly, enrolled full time during some part of each of five calendar months of the year);
- not be claimed as a dependent on someone else's return;
- be a US resident for tax purposes (nonresident aliens generally do not qualify, and residents of US territories claim through their territory's tax agency, not the IRS);
- have made a contribution to an eligible retirement plan or IRA.

The fourth point carries a caveat. Notice 2026-48 says Treasury expects to address how a 1996 welfare law that restricts "federal public benefits" applies to the match. That could exclude some non-citizens who are otherwise tax residents. Nothing is final.

**You do not need to owe income tax.** The IRS confirms that a small or zero federal income tax bill does not rule you out, which is the whole point of switching from a credit to a deposit.

### The 2027 income limits

The income test uses **modified adjusted gross income (MAGI)**. For married couples filing jointly, it is the couple's combined MAGI. These are the limits for 2027 published by the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match):

| Filing status | Full 50% match if MAGI is | Reduced match if MAGI is | No match if MAGI is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Married filing jointly, qualifying surviving spouse | $41,000 or less | $41,001 to $70,999 | $71,000 or more |
| Head of household | $30,750 or less | $30,751 to $53,249 | $53,250 or more |
| Single, married filing separately | $20,500 or less | $20,501 to $35,499 | $35,500 or more |

These figures are written into the statute for 2027, so they are not waiting on an inflation update. The IRS says the limits will be adjusted for inflation in later years, and the statute rounds that adjustment to the nearest $1,000. The width of each reduction band stays the same.

### Watch the "modified" in modified AGI

This is the detail most likely to catch people out. MAGI for the Saver's Match starts with your adjusted gross income and then **adds back** your pre-tax retirement contributions and deductible IRA contributions, along with some foreign income exclusions. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) and the [statute](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/html/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleF-chap65-subchapB-sec6433.htm) both spell this out.

Say you are single, earn $22,000 and put $2,000 into a pre-tax 401(k). On paper your AGI is about $20,000. For the match, though, the $2,000 goes back in, so MAGI is about $22,000, above the full-match line.

**You cannot contribute your way down into a better bracket.** If your income sits close to a threshold, work from MAGI, not the AGI line on last year's return.

## How much you'd get: worked examples

Below the first threshold the match is simple: half of what you contribute, counting up to $2,000. Contribute $600 and you get $300; contribute $2,000 or more and you get $1,000.

Inside the reduction band it takes one formula. Notice 2026-48 sets it out like this:

1. Take your MAGI minus the full-match threshold for your filing status.
2. Divide by the width of the band: $15,000 for single filers, $22,500 for heads of household, $30,000 for joint filers.
3. Multiply by 50. That is the number of percentage points the match drops.
4. Round **down** to a whole number and subtract it from 50%. The result is your match rate.
5. Apply that rate to your contributions, up to $2,000.

Rounding down the reduction works slightly in your favour. The Notice's own example shows how: a single filer with MAGI of $30,000 has a reduction of 31.67 points, which becomes 31, leaving a 19% rate.

### Five profiles

The first, third and fourth rows are our own calculations using that formula; the second and fifth are the worked examples in [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-48.pdf). None of them accounts for earlier withdrawals, which can reduce the contributions that count (more on that below).

| Profile | Filing status | MAGI | Contributes in 2027 | Match rate | Saver's Match |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Part-time retail worker | Single | $18,000 | $1,200 to a 401(k) | 50% | $600 |
| Notice 2026-48, Example 1 | Single | $30,000 | $1,500 to a traditional IRA | 19% | $285 |
| Single parent | Head of household | $45,000 | $2,000 to a 403(b) | 19% | $380 |
| Couple, both working | Married filing jointly | $55,000 | $2,000 and $600 | 27% | $540 + $162 = $702 |
| Notice 2026-48, Example 2 | Married filing jointly | $63,000 | $1,000 and $2,000 | 14% | $140 + $280 = $420 |

How the head of household number works: $45,000 minus $30,750 is $14,250; divided by $22,500 that is 0.633; times 50 is 31.67, rounded down to 31; 50 minus 31 leaves 19%; 19% of $2,000 is $380.

The couple at $55,000: $55,000 minus $41,000 is $14,000; divided by $30,000 is 0.467; times 50 is 23.33, rounded down to 23; the rate is 27%. One spouse gets 27% of $2,000, the other 27% of $600. Each spouse's match is worked out separately and goes into that spouse's own account, but both use the couple's combined MAGI.

>> **Tip for couples:** the $2,000 cap is per person. If one spouse saves $4,000 and the other saves nothing, only $2,000 counts. Splitting the same $4,000 so each spouse puts in $2,000 doubles the amount that is matched, as long as both are otherwise eligible.

### Withdrawals can shrink the contributions that count

The statute reduces your qualifying contributions by any distributions you took from retirement accounts during a "testing period": the tax year itself, the two years before it, and the time up to your return's due date, including extensions. Loans, rollovers and the return of excess contributions are ignored. For joint filers, a spouse's distributions count too, according to the [statute](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title26/html/USCODE-2024-title26-subtitleF-chap65-subchapB-sec6433.htm).

So if you contributed $2,000 in 2027 but took $1,500 out of an old 401(k) in 2026, only $500 counts. The rule exists to stop people cycling the same money in and out to collect the match, and it is the one most likely to surprise someone who cashed out a former employer's plan.

## Which accounts qualify, and do you need to open one?

Two different lists matter here, and mixing them up is the most common misunderstanding.

**Contributions that count toward the match** are broad. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) lists 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457(b) plans, plus traditional and Roth IRAs. Notice 2026-48 also counts SIMPLE IRA and SEP deferrals and certain voluntary after-tax contributions. ABLE accounts do not count toward the match.

**Accounts that can receive the match** are narrower. The statute and Notice 2026-48 limit deposits to non-Roth accounts: a 401(k), a salary-reduction 403(b), a governmental 457(b), the federal Thrift Savings Plan, or a traditional IRA. Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k) money cannot receive the match directly.

The receiving account also has to accept it, and that is **voluntary**. Under Notice 2026-48, no employer plan or IRA provider is obliged to take Saver's Match deposits; Treasury simply urges them to. A plan that opts in has to amend its documents, and it can later stop accepting the deposits.

### So, do you need to open an account?

It depends on what you already have:

- **You save in a workplace 401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b).** Ask HR or the plan administrator whether the plan will accept Saver's Match contributions in 2028. If yes, you may not need anything else. Treasury is still choosing between three ways to route money to employer plans, so the exact process is not settled.
- **You save only in a Roth IRA.** Your contributions count, but the match cannot land there directly. Notice 2026-48 proposes that Treasury pays into a traditional "conduit" IRA, which can then be moved to your Roth. That move is a Roth conversion, so the amount becomes taxable income that year and may be subject to tax withholding. You could instead keep a separate traditional IRA open just to receive the match.
- **You have no retirement account.** You will need to open one and contribute in 2027. If you plan to use a traditional IRA, Notice 2026-48 proposes that you open it with a provider that has registered with the IRS, before you file, and get an "IRA tracking number" to enter on Form 8880-A.

That last point is where TrumpIRA.gov fits in. [Executive Order 14403](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/05/2026-08908/promoting-retirement-savings-access-for-american-workers-by-establishing-trumpiragov), signed on April 30, 2026 and summarised in [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-48.pdf), directs Treasury to set up a website by January 1, 2027 with information on low-cost, high-quality IRAs.

The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) says that starting in 2027, TrumpIRA.gov will list financial institutions that offer IRAs, accept Saver's Match contributions and meet other criteria. Trade association [ASPPA reported](https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/9/trumpira.gov-live--offers-info-on-accounts-matching) in September that an early version was live with general IRA information and a link to sign up for updates, but no provider list. Nothing in the official guidance makes using the site a condition for the match; it works as a directory.

What if your account can't take the deposit, or you name the wrong one? Nobody knows yet. Notice 2026-48 does not say what happens to a claim with no valid destination. Money sent to the wrong kind of account is treated as an erroneous payment, which the IRS can recover as if it were unpaid tax, without accuracy-related or fraud penalties. Until the final rules arrive, confirm before you file that the receiving account exists, is in your name and accepts the match.

## How to claim it and when the money arrives

You claim the match on **Form 8880-A, Saver's Match for Qualified Retirement Savings Contributions**, filed with your 2027 federal return in 2028. Notice 2026-48 describes it as a new form that has not yet been published. On it you will give the details of the account that should receive the deposit, and you can choose the refundable credit if your match is under $100. Contributions to an ABLE account stay on the existing Form 8880.

A few timing rules matter:

- **Workplace plan contributions** have to come out of your pay by December 31, 2027.
- **IRA contributions for 2027** can be made up to the April 2028 filing deadline, not counting extensions, according to [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-48.pdf). If you miss it, the contribution counts for 2028 instead.
- **You have to file a return.** If your income is low enough that you normally don't file, the match is a reason to file.

After that, you wait. With no published payment window yet, keep your copy of Form 8880-A so you can check the deposit against it when it arrives.

## Saver's Match vs Saver's Credit

Until the end of 2026, the federal reward for low-income savers was the Saver's Credit. The match replaces it for retirement contributions from 2027, according to the [IRS news release](https://www.irs.gov/node/163436). The two differ in more ways than the name.

| | Saver's Credit (through tax year 2026) | Saver's Match (from tax year 2027) |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | A tax credit that reduces the income tax you owe | A federal deposit into a retirement account |
| If you owe no income tax | Nonrefundable, so you could get nothing | You can still qualify; tax owed doesn't matter |
| Rate | Set in tiers that step down as income rises | 50%, sliding smoothly to 0% across the reduction band |
| Top income limit, married filing jointly | $80,500 (2026) | $71,000 (2027) |
| Top income limit, head of household | $60,375 (2026) | $53,250 (2027) |
| Top income limit, single | $40,250 (2026) | $35,500 (2027) |
| Form | 8880 | 8880-A |
| Roth IRA contributions | Count | Count, but the match can't be deposited into a Roth |
| ABLE account contributions | Count | Don't count; the Saver's Credit continues for ABLE contributions |
| Where the money ends up | Your tax refund or lower bill, yours to spend | Inside a retirement account, under retirement rules |

Sources: 2026 Saver's Credit limits from IRS announcement [IR-2025-111](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USIRS/bulletins/3fb3d4b); match limits and the ABLE rule from the [IRS Saver's Match page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match); the credit's nonrefundable status as reported by [The College Investor](https://thecollegeinvestor.com/85989/treasury-and-irs-detail-1000-savers-match-coming-to-retirement-accounts-in-2027/).

Two things stand out. First, a nonrefundable credit is worth nothing to a household with no income tax bill, and lower-income savers are the group most likely to be in that position. The match fixes that. Second, the top income limits are **lower** for the match than they were for the credit in 2026. A household near the top of the old credit's range, for example a single filer on $38,000, could have claimed some Saver's Credit for 2026 but gets no match in 2027. If that is you, your 2026 return is the last chance to claim the credit.

## Taxes and withdrawals: the strings attached

The match is not free spending money, and it helps to know the rules before it arrives.

**Taxes.** Notice 2026-48 says the match is not included in your income when it is deposited. It is treated like a pre-tax contribution, so when you eventually withdraw it, and whatever it has earned, that amount is taxed as ordinary income in the year you take it out. Rolling it over to another eligible account does not trigger tax.

**Early withdrawals.** Taking money out before age 59½ can cost you extra tax and run into limits, the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) cautions. There are two layers. The usual 10% additional tax on early distributions can apply.

On top of that, the statute creates a "Saver's Match recovery tax": if you take an early distribution and, at the end of the year, the total Saver's Match ever paid into that account is more than the account's balance, the excess is added to your tax bill (reduced by the 10% tax you paid and by investment losses). You can cut that tax by putting the money back by your return's due date, including extensions. In effect, the government gets its match back if you drain the account early.

**Hardship withdrawals.** In a 401(k), 403(b) or 457(b), the match itself cannot be taken out as a hardship or unforeseeable-emergency withdrawal, according to Notice 2026-48. The earnings on it can. This is a good reason not to treat a retirement account as your safety net. If you are building savings from scratch, it pays to keep a separate cash cushion as well; our guide on [how to build an emergency fund](/articles/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund) covers how big it should be.

>> **Common mistake:** contributing in 2027 and then withdrawing the money a few months later. The withdrawal reduces your qualifying contributions for the match, and money already matched can be clawed back through the recovery tax. Only contribute what you can leave alone.

## What we don't know yet

Notice 2026-48 is a statement of intent, not final rules. Proposed regulations are still to come, and comments closed on October 5, 2026. As of November 10, 2026, these questions are open:

- **How the money reaches employer plans.** Treasury has floated three routes (plans registering, an automatic system based on plan data, or the saver moving the money themselves from a conduit IRA). None has been picked.
- **The IRA registration process.** How providers sign up, how you get an IRA tracking number, and whether TrumpIRA.gov will be part of that process are still being decided.
- **What happens if a deposit fails.** The Notice does not say whether a claim with no valid account is refunded, held, or lost.
- **Which residents qualify.** Treasury expects to address how federal-benefit rules for non-citizens apply.
- **Payment timing and the form.** "As soon as practicable" has no number attached, and Form 8880-A and its instructions have not been published.
- **Which providers and plans will accept the match.** Participation is voluntary, so coverage will depend on how many sign up.
- **The 2028 income limits.** They will move with inflation, but the figures have not been published.

We will update this article as the IRS publishes the proposed regulations and the form.

## A timeline checklist: what to do and when

### Before December 31, 2026

1. If you contributed to a retirement account in 2026 and your income is under the Saver's Credit limits, plan to claim the credit on Form 8880 with your 2026 return. It is the last year for retirement contributions.
2. Work out roughly where your 2027 MAGI will land: take your expected income and add back any pre-tax 401(k) or deductible IRA contributions.
3. If you have a workplace plan, check whether you are enrolled and what percentage you contribute. Reaching $2,000 a year is about $77 per biweekly paycheck.
4. Avoid taking money out of retirement accounts if you can. Withdrawals in 2025, 2026 and 2027 can all reduce your 2027 match. If a job loss or a downturn is what you are worried about, [what a recession means for your money](/articles/what-a-recession-means-for-your-money) covers the steps to take before you reach for retirement savings.

### During 2027

1. Contribute at least enough to reach the amount you want matched, up to $2,000 per person. Couples may want to split contributions so each spouse can reach their own cap.
2. Ask your plan administrator whether the plan will accept Saver's Match deposits.
3. If you rely on a Roth IRA or have no account, decide where the match should go. Watch for the IRS registration process and the provider list on TrumpIRA.gov.
4. Keep records of every contribution, including year-end 401(k) statements.

### When you file in 2028

1. Make any last 2027 IRA contribution before the April 2028 deadline (no extensions).
2. File your 2027 return even if you normally wouldn't need to.
3. Attach Form 8880-A, enter the receiving account details exactly, and decide whether to take a match under $100 as a credit.
4. Check that the deposit arrives, and leave it invested. If you are not sure how to invest it once it lands, [index funds explained for beginners](/articles/index-funds-explained-for-beginners) is a sensible place to start.

If your situation is unusual (a recent move from abroad, a mix of Roth and pre-tax accounts, a large withdrawal in recent years), check the [IRS Saver's Match page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) or ask a tax professional before you rely on a specific amount.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need an IRA to get the Saver's Match?

No, if your workplace 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b) or the TSP accepts the deposit. You do need some eligible account that will receive it, and it cannot be a Roth account. If you only have a Roth IRA or no account at all, you will likely need a traditional IRA with a provider that accepts the match.

### Is the Saver's Match taxable?

Not when it goes in. It is taxed as ordinary income when you withdraw it, and early withdrawals before 59½ can bring the 10% additional tax and the Saver's Match recovery tax.

### Can I get the Saver's Match and the Saver's Credit at the same time?

Not for the same retirement contributions. From 2027 the match replaces the credit for retirement savings. The credit survives only for contributions to ABLE accounts.

## The takeaway

For someone earning under $20,500, or a couple under $41,000, the Saver's Match is a 50% return on the first $2,000 each person saves in 2027. Very few things in personal finance come close to that. Above those levels the match shrinks quickly, so run the formula on your own numbers rather than counting on the headline $1,000.

The honest answer to "is it worth it?" depends less on the match than on whether you can leave the money alone. The rules still being written are mostly plumbing, about how the money finds your account. What you can do now is check your 2027 MAGI against the table, and if you plan to take part, contributions can start in January 2027.
