Somebody at work, or a video in your feed, has told you that from next year the government will put up to $1,000 a year into your retirement account. You earn around $28,000, or you and your partner bring in about $60,000 between you, and you have two questions. Is that real? And if it is, how much of the $1,000 would actually reach you?

It is real. It is called the Saver's Match, it was written into law in the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022, and it starts with retirement contributions made in 2027. But the headline number is the ceiling, not the typical payment. The match shrinks quickly as income rises, and some savers will pick an account that cannot receive it.

>> **In short:** the match is real, but $1,000 is the ceiling; the payment shrinks fast as income rises, and it can only land in a pre-tax account.
>> - The Saver's Match is a federal deposit of up to 50% of your retirement contributions, on up to $2,000 a year per person, so the maximum is **$1,000 per person**.
>> - It counts contributions made in **2027**. You claim it on your 2027 tax return, filed in **2028**, with a new **Form 8880-A**. There is no match for 2026 contributions, but withdrawals you make in 2026 can shrink your 2027 match.
>> - The full 50% rate stops at a modified AGI of $20,500 (single), $30,750 (head of household) or $41,000 (married filing jointly). The match ends at $35,500, $53,250 and $71,000.
>> - Roth IRA contributions **count**, but the government's money **cannot be paid straight into a Roth IRA** or the Roth part of a 401(k).
>> - It replaces the Saver's Credit for retirement contributions and, unlike that credit, is paid even if you owe no income tax.

## What the Saver's Match is

The Saver's Match comes from section 103 of SECURE 2.0, which added section 6433 to the tax code. It replaces the Saver's Credit, a tax credit for lower-income people who save for retirement. The [IRS Saver's Match page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) sums up the idea: for each dollar you save, within set limits, the government adds as much as 50 cents, capped at $1,000 per person per year. That money is paid into a retirement account you choose and is then invested together with the rest of your balance.

**That last part is the real change.** The old credit reduced your tax bill, which was worth nothing if you owed no federal income tax. The match is a deposit instead, so according to the IRS a small or zero tax bill does not stop you from getting it.

In August 2026 the Treasury and the IRS released [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB), a question-and-answer document published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-35 (dated August 24, 2026). It is the most detailed official description of how the match will work, but as its title says, it sets out what the agencies *intend* to put into regulations. Comments closed on October 5, 2026, and we had not found proposed regulations when we checked, so some details below could still change. We flag those as we go.

The program also has a political brand. The notice refers to Executive Order 14403 of April 30, 2026, which led to a government website, TrumpIRA.gov, covered further down. A "Trump IRA" is not a separate type of account; the rules are the ones in section 6433.

## Who qualifies for the Saver's Match

There are two layers: basic conditions about who you are, and an income test.

### The basic conditions

According to the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) and [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB), you must meet all of these for the tax year:

1. **You contribute to an eligible retirement plan or IRA.** There is no minimum amount.
2. **You are at least 18** by the end of the year.
3. **You are not a full-time student** under the tax code definition: roughly, enrolled full-time during some part of each of five calendar months.
4. **Nobody else claims you as a dependent.**
5. **You are a US resident for tax purposes.** Bona fide residents of US territories have to claim through their territory's tax agency.

The student rule catches more people than you might expect. A 22-year-old working part-time while studying full-time for most of the year will not qualify that year, even with a low income and a 401(k). The year after graduation is a different story.

### The income test: modified AGI, not your take-home pay

The match depends on your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) and filing status, and here is the detail most summaries skip. For the Saver's Match, MAGI is *higher* than the AGI on your return. The notice builds it from four pieces: your AGI, **plus** whatever you put into workplace retirement plans pre-tax through payroll, **plus** any traditional IRA contributions you deducted, plus certain foreign and territorial income that was left out of AGI. The [IRS page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) says the same in shorter form.

**So saving pre-tax in a 401(k) does not move you down the scale.** Say you earn $30,000 and defer $2,000 pre-tax. Your AGI may drop to $28,000, but your MAGI for the match is still about $30,000. For most wage earners, MAGI will sit close to gross pay plus other income such as interest, unemployment benefits or side-gig profit.

The 2027 limits:

| Filing status | Full 50% match if MAGI is | Reduced match if MAGI is | No match if MAGI is | Phase-out range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single, married filing separately | $20,500 or less | $20,501 to $35,499 | $35,500 or more | $15,000 |
| Head of household | $30,750 or less | $30,751 to $53,249 | $53,250 or more | $22,500 |
| Married filing jointly, qualifying surviving spouse | $41,000 or less | $41,001 to $70,999 | $71,000 or more | $30,000 |

*Sources: [IRS Saver's Match page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) for the bands; [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB) for the phase-out ranges.*

### Will the limits rise with inflation?

Partly. The IRS says the income limits [will be adjusted for inflation in years after 2027](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match). According to the notice, the thresholds and maximum MAGI amounts are indexed, but the *width* of each phase-out range and the $2,000 contribution cap are not. The $1,000 maximum stays fixed in dollars while the income window shifts a little each year. The 2028 figures have not been published.

### Married couples

On a joint return, eligibility is checked for each spouse separately, but the MAGI used is the couple's combined figure. Each spouse can have up to $2,000 of their own contributions matched, so a couple under $41,000 where both contribute $2,000 could receive $2,000 between them. If only one of you contributes, only that person gets a match.

## How much you'll get: the formula and worked examples

Below the threshold, the match is 50% of contributions, on up to $2,000. Between the threshold and the cut-off, the rate falls on a sliding scale. The IRS web page only says the rate decreases as income rises; the method is in Q&A C-2 of [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB):

1. Subtract the threshold for your filing status ($20,500, $30,750 or $41,000) from your MAGI and divide by your phase-out range ($15,000, $22,500 or $30,000).
2. Multiply by 50 to get the percentage points to take off.
3. **Round down** to a whole point. This helps you, because fewer points come off.
4. Subtract from 50%. That is your match rate.
5. Multiply your contributions, up to $2,000, by that rate.

The notice's own example: a single filer with MAGI of $30,000 puts $1,500 into an IRA. ($30,000 − $20,500) ÷ $15,000 × 50 = 31.67, rounded down to 31. The rate is 50% − 31 = 19%, and the match is $1,500 × 19% = **$285**.

Here is the same method for the kinds of households the program targets. These are hypothetical people, not predictions of anyone's payment.

### Single, MAGI $28,000

- Points off: ($28,000 − $20,500) ÷ $15,000 × 50 = 25.
- Match rate: 50% − 25 = **25%**.
- Contributes $2,000 (about $77 per paycheck if paid every two weeks): $2,000 × 25% = **$500**.
- Contributes $1,000: **$250**.

The person who hears "$1,000 from the government" and earns $28,000 should expect half that, even saving the full $2,000.

### Married filing jointly, combined MAGI $60,000

- Points off: ($60,000 − $41,000) ÷ $30,000 × 50 = 31.67, rounded down to 31.
- Match rate: 50% − 31 = **19%**.
- Partner A contributes $2,000 to a 401(k): **$380**.
- Partner B contributes $600 to an IRA: $600 × 19% = **$114**.
- Household total: **$494**. If Partner B also saved $2,000, it would be $760.

### Head of household, MAGI $40,000

A single parent filing as head of household gets a wider band, which works in their favour here. ($40,000 − $30,750) ÷ $22,500 × 50 = 20.56, rounded down to 20 points off, so the rate is 50% − 20 = **30%**. Putting in $1,500 brings a match of $1,500 × 30% = **$450**, more than the single filer at $28,000 gets on the same money.

### Single, near the top of the range

At MAGI of $35,000: ($14,500 ÷ $15,000) × 50 = 48.33, rounded down to 48, so the rate is 2% and $2,000 of contributions earns **$40**. Under Q&A D-2 of the notice, a match that would be **under $100** can instead be taken as a refundable tax credit, which helps if you have no account that accepts the deposit.

| Example | Filing status | MAGI | Match rate | Contribution | Match |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low income | Single | $20,000 | 50% | $2,000 | $1,000 |
| Mid-range | Single | $28,000 | 25% | $2,000 | $500 |
| Notice example | Single | $30,000 | 19% | $1,500 | $285 |
| Near the top | Single | $35,000 | 2% | $2,000 | $40 |
| Single parent | Head of household | $40,000 | 30% | $1,500 | $450 |
| Couple, both saving | Married filing jointly | $60,000 | 19% | $2,000 + $600 | $494 |

*Hypothetical households. Rates calculated with the formula in Q&A C-2 of [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB) and the 2027 limits.*

### Recent withdrawals shrink what gets matched

There is a trap in the counting. **Under the notice, money taken out of retirement accounts during the year, up to the filing deadline, *and in the two previous years* is subtracted from your contributions first.**

The notice's own example shows how fast that bites. Someone who defers $2,000 in 2027 but withdrew $500 in 2027 and $900 in 2026, without rolling either over, has only $600 of matchable contributions. Rollovers, returned excess contributions and some plan loans do not count against you, so if you are thinking of cashing out an old 401(k) after a job change, roll it over instead.

## Which accounts count, and the Roth catch

Two separate questions get mixed up here: which contributions *earn* a match, and which accounts can *receive* it.

### Contributions that earn the match

The list of contributions that count is broad, up to $2,000 a year per person. What gets left out is mostly money that was already in the system.

| Counts toward the match | Does not count |
|---|---|
| Contributions to a **traditional or Roth IRA** | Rollovers and transfers |
| Elective deferrals to a **401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b)** | The match itself |
| Salary-reduction contributions to a SIMPLE IRA or SEP | **ABLE account** contributions (these may still qualify for the Saver's Credit) |
| Voluntary after-tax contributions to a qualified plan | |
| Contributions to 501(c)(18) plans | |

*Sources: [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB); [IRS Saver's Match page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) for ABLE accounts.*

IRA savers get extra time: IRA contributions for 2027 can be made up to the 2028 filing deadline if you designate them for 2027, so you can see your final income before deciding how much to put in.

### Accounts that can receive the match

This is the catch. Under Q&A D-1 of the notice, the government's deposit can go only into:

- A **traditional IRA** (any IRA that is not a Roth IRA), or
- A **401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b)** plan, but **not** its Roth portion.

On top of that, the account has to be held for your benefit, its provider or plan has to have agreed to take match money, and you have to name it as the destination through whatever process the Treasury sets up. The match is pre-tax money: not taxed going in, taxed as income when withdrawn, and not counted against your annual IRA or 401(k) limits.

>> **Watch out:** "I opened a Roth IRA, so I'm all set" is an easy mistake to make. Your Roth contributions count toward the match, but the match cannot be paid directly into a Roth IRA.

Say you are single, earn $28,000, and put $2,000 into a Roth IRA during 2027. You have earned a $500 match. If the Roth is your only account, though, that $500 has no approved place to go.

The notice describes a workaround under consideration: the Treasury would set up a *conduit* traditional IRA for you and move the money to your Roth by trustee-to-trustee transfer, treated as a Roth conversion, which normally means the converted amount counts as taxable income. That route is a plan, not a working system. **The simplest path is to have, or open before you file in 2028, a traditional IRA with a provider that accepts the match, or a pre-tax workplace account that does.**

### Your employer's plan might not accept it

Workplace plans are not obliged to take the match. The notice leaves the choice to each employer. Taking match money requires a plan **amendment** that nobody is forced to adopt; an employer that opts in can put limits on it, for example only for people still on the payroll; and it can later amend the plan again to stop taking the match going forward. If you work for a small employer or change jobs often, an IRA is probably the more dependable home, because it follows you rather than your job.

## How to claim it and when the money arrives

The timeline is longer than most people assume. **Money you save in 2027 is matched in 2028 at the earliest**, and only if you ask for it.

| When | What happens | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 2026 | Nothing for the match, [says the IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match). 2026 contributions still fall under the Saver's Credit | Keep saving; claim the credit as usual if you qualify; avoid retirement withdrawals you don't need, since they count against the 2027 match |
| During 2027 | Contributions start counting | Contribute to an eligible plan or IRA and keep records |
| Up to the 2028 filing deadline | IRA contributions can still be designated for 2027 | Top up an IRA if you have room |
| When you file your 2027 return | You claim the match with **Form 8880-A**. The IRS says you do need to apply; nothing arrives automatically | Attach the form |
| After you file | The notice quotes the law: the match is paid "as soon as practicable" after a return claiming it is filed. No payment date has been announced | Watch for the deposit |

Form 8880-A had not been published when the notice came out, and we have not seen a final version. The notice sketches how you would point the money at an account. For a traditional IRA, you would enter an *IRA tracking number* from a registered provider on your return, so the IRA would need to exist before you file. For workplace plans, the Treasury is weighing three routes: plans register and receive the money through a conduit IRA; plan and participant data are matched automatically; or you get a confirmation number to give your plan. Until that is settled, the useful steps are boring ones: contribute, keep statements, and watch for messages from your plan or IRA provider.

### The overlap for IRA savers in early 2027

According to the notice, an IRA contribution made between January 1, 2027 and the due date of your 2026 return can be designated either as a **2026** contribution, for the old Saver's Credit, or as a **2027** contribution, for the match. Not both. If you have little or no tax for a credit to offset, the match is usually worth more.

## Saver's Match vs the old Saver's Credit

On paper the two look alike: the same $2,000 contribution base and the same $1,000 ceiling per person. In practice they behave very differently.

| | Saver's Credit (retirement contributions through 2026) | Saver's Match (from 2027) |
|---|---|---|
| What you get | A credit that lowers your federal income tax | A deposit into a retirement account |
| If you owe no tax | Worth nothing; the credit is nonrefundable | Still paid |
| Maximum | $1,000 per person, $2,000 on a joint return | $1,000 per person, $2,000 on a joint return if both contribute |
| Rate | Fixed tiers from 50% down to 10% | 50%, reduced on a sliding scale |
| Income cut-off | AGI above $40,250 single, $60,375 head of household, $80,500 joint (2026 limits, its final year) | MAGI of $35,500 single, $53,250 head of household, $71,000 joint (2027 limits) |
| Where the money goes | Your tax bill | Traditional IRA or pre-tax workplace plan |
| Form | 8880 | 8880-A |
| ABLE accounts | Eligible | Not eligible (the credit still applies) |

*Sources: [IRS 2026 limits announcement](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500), [IRS Publication 590-A (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) and [Tax Topic 610](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc610) for the Saver's Credit; [IRS Saver's Match page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) and [Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB) for the match.*

The income rows are not like-for-like, since they cover different years and the match adds pre-tax contributions back into income. The direction is still clear. The match is a better deal for people with very low tax bills, who often got little or nothing from the credit. It can be worse for people near the top of the old credit's range, who may now fall outside it, and for anyone who wants all their savings in Roth accounts.

Our honest read: the match fixes the credit's biggest flaw, which was that it helped least the people it was meant to help most. The cost is complexity. A credit happened on one tax form; the match involves a tax form, a registered provider and an account that has opted in, and each extra step is a place where eligible people can drop out.

## Withdrawals, clawbacks and mistaken payments

Once deposited, the match follows the normal rules for pre-tax retirement money, plus a few additions from the notice.

**Taxes and the 10% penalty.** Withdrawals of match money are taxed as income, and under age 59½ the usual 10% additional tax on early distributions may apply.

**The recovery tax.** The law adds a "Saver's Match recovery tax" aimed at people who take the match and pull it back out. In outline: if you take an early distribution from an account that received match money and, at year-end, the account holds less than the total match it received, an extra tax applies to the shortfall. It is reduced by the 10% early distribution tax and by investment losses, and further by any money you put back by your return's due date. The Treasury asked for comments on how to measure those losses, so the mechanics are not final.

**No hardship withdrawals of the match.** Match money received from the Treasury cannot be taken out of a workplace plan as a hardship or unforeseeable-emergency distribution.

**Mistaken payments.** If the Treasury later decides a match was paid in error, it is treated as an underpayment of tax. If you take the money out by that year's return due date, the notice says it is not taxed (apart from earnings) and the 10% additional tax does not apply.

The lesson is the usual one: this is long-term money. If you may need cash in the next year or two, build an [emergency fund](/articles/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund) first.

## What TrumpIRA.gov is, and how to avoid scams

TrumpIRA.gov is the government website linked to the executive order. According to [ASPPA](https://www.asppa-net.org/news/2026/9/trumpira.gov-live--offers-info-on-accounts-matching), it was already live by early September 2026, offering general IRA information pitched at people whose job comes with no retirement plan, contractors, part-timers and the self-employed among them. The Treasury plans its official launch for January 1, 2027.

The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) says that from 2027 the site is meant to act as a directory of IRA providers that have agreed to take match deposits and pass further tests. What those tests are, and which firms will make the list, had not been published when we checked.

Do you need to use it? Based on what has been published, no. It is a directory and information hub. The match is claimed on your tax return and paid to an account at a provider; if your current IRA provider or workplace plan accepts it, nothing requires you to go through the site.

>> **Scam warning:** a new government payment with a famous name attached is ideal bait. Expect texts and emails offering to "release your $1,000 Trump IRA bonus" for a fee or your Social Security number. The real match is claimed on your tax return, and nobody can speed it up for a fee. According to the IRS, it [won't email or text you unless you have agreed to it, and it doesn't contact people through social media messages](https://www.irs.gov/help/report-fraud/report-fake-irs-treasury-or-tax-related-emails-and-messages). Forward suspicious messages to phishing@irs.gov, and see our guide to [spotting phishing emails and scam texts](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-emails-and-scam-texts).

## What we don't know yet

As of November 2026, these points were still open. We will update this article as official guidance arrives.

- **The final rules.** Proposed and then final regulations still have to follow the notice, and anything drawn from it could change.
- **Form 8880-A** and its instructions.
- **How you point the money at your account:** IRA tracking numbers, plan registration and confirmation numbers are options, not settled procedures.
- **Which providers and plans will accept the match.** Neither list exists yet.
- **The Roth route** through a conduit IRA, which is only a proposal.
- **Payment dates.** "As soon as practicable" after filing could mean weeks or months in 2028.
- **2028 income limits**, which will be published later.

## What to do now: a checklist

1. **Estimate your 2027 MAGI.** Start from expected gross pay plus other income, without subtracting pre-tax 401(k) contributions, and compare it with the table above.
2. **Check the basic conditions:** 18 or older by the end of 2027, not a full-time student, not someone's dependent, a US tax resident.
3. **Decide where your own contributions go.** Small amounts count: the [IRS example](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/savers-match) shows $20 a month, $240 a year, earning a $120 match at the full rate.
4. **Make sure the match has somewhere to land:** a traditional IRA or a pre-tax workplace account that accepts it. Ask HR during 2027 whether your plan is opting in. If you only have a Roth IRA, consider opening a traditional IRA alongside it before you file in 2028.
5. **Leave existing retirement money alone.** Withdrawals in 2025, 2026 and 2027 reduce what gets matched.
6. **Aim for $2,000 if you can**, the most that gets matched. For money you invest yourself, our guide to [index funds](/articles/index-funds-explained-for-beginners) explains how to pick a low-cost fund.
7. **Keep records**: pay stubs showing deferrals, IRA contribution confirmations, year-end statements.
8. **Claim it in 2028** with Form 8880-A. If you use free filing software or a volunteer tax clinic, ask about it by name.
9. **Ignore anyone who wants a fee to get you the match.**

## The takeaway

If you only do one thing this month, work out your likely 2027 MAGI and see which band of the table you fall into. That number tells you whether the match is worth planning around or just a small bonus.

If it is worth it, the next step is a quick look at your accounts. Check whether you hold a traditional IRA or a pre-tax workplace account, and set a contribution you can keep up through 2027, even $20 a month. The forms, provider lists and payment dates are still being written, and we will update this guide as they are.
