You hear "inflation has cooled" on the evening news, then you pay for a cart of groceries that cost noticeably less five years ago. Your rent renewal came in higher again. The car insurance bill went up even though you haven't filed a claim in a decade. Either the news is wrong or something about the way inflation is reported doesn't match your life.

Neither, really. The headline figure answers a different question from the one you're asking at the checkout. It tells you how *fast* prices are rising, not how *high* they are, which is why prices stay high even when inflation slows.

Once that clicks, the next inflation report makes a lot more sense. This guide covers why prices almost never return to where they were, which ones do fall, whether pay has kept up, and how to work out your own inflation rate, which is often not the official one.

>> **In short:** inflation is the speed at which prices climb, not the price level itself.
>> - When inflation slows, prices keep rising, just more slowly. They only fall when inflation turns negative, which is called deflation.
>> - US consumer prices were about 26% higher in December 2025 than in December 2019, and they've risen further in 2026.
>> - Some goods do get cheaper (eggs, gasoline, TVs). Services like rent and insurance almost never do.
>> - The useful question isn't "when will prices go back down?" but "is my income rising faster than my costs?"

## Why prices stay high when inflation slows: speed vs altitude

Picture a plane climbing. Inflation is its rate of climb: how many feet it gains each minute. The price level is the altitude.

If the pilot eases off from a steep climb to a gentle one, the plane is still going up. It certainly doesn't drop back to the runway.

That's what happened to US prices after 2022. The rate of climb fell sharply, but the plane kept gaining height every year. The St. Louis Fed makes the same point with a car slowing from 70 to 40 mph: [it's still moving forward](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2025/july/differences-prices-inflation-explained), just less quickly.

Economists call that slowdown **disinflation**, and it's easy to confuse with **deflation**; the side-by-side table further down separates the two. The St. Louis Fed's explainer is blunt about it: a falling inflation rate [does not mean prices are falling](https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/page-one-economics/2024/03/01/the-inflation-rate-is-falling-but-prices-are-not?print=true). As long as the rate stays above zero, even at 1% or 2%, the price level keeps setting new highs.

## A worked example: one basket from 2019 to 2026

Take a basket of goods and services that cost exactly $100 in December 2019, and let its price move in line with the overall CPI. Using the BLS index values above, here's what the same basket would have cost each December (a Guide Market calculation, rounded to the cent):

| Date | Inflation over the previous 12 months | Cost of the same basket |
|---|---|---|
| Dec 2019 | 2.3% | $100.00 |
| Dec 2020 | 1.4% | $101.36 |
| Dec 2021 | 7.0% | $108.49 |
| Dec 2022 | 6.5% | $115.50 |
| Dec 2023 | 3.4% | $119.37 |
| Dec 2024 | 2.9% | $122.82 |
| Dec 2025 | 2.7% | $126.10 |
| Oct 2026 | not published (see below) | $131.34 |

Two things stand out. First, the "good" years still added money. Going from 6.5% to 3.4% to 2.9% felt like progress in the news, and it was, but the basket still got more expensive every single year. Second, the increases compound: 2023's rise was applied to a basket that already cost $115.50, not $100.

The last row matters too. Between December 2025 and October 2026 the index rose from 324.054 to 337.511, about 4.2% in ten months before seasonal adjustment. So 2026 has not been a year of cooling inflation. As our guide to [how Fed interest rate changes affect you](/articles/how-fed-interest-rate-changes-affect-you) explains, the Fed's [October 2026 statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm) still described inflation as elevated, and two officials voted to raise rates.

>> **The key idea:** whatever inflation does next, the basket won't go back to $100 unless prices actually *fall* for years, which is deflation. Disinflation only slows the climb from wherever you are now.

## Inflation, disinflation and deflation side by side

The three terms get mixed up because they describe the same thing, how the price level moves, from different angles. Here they are with real examples.

| Term | What happens to prices | A real example |
|---|---|---|
| Inflation | The price level rises | All items CPI rose 7.0% in the 12 months to December 2021 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/consumer-price-index-2025-in-review.htm)) |
| Disinflation | Prices still rise, but more slowly than before | Inflation went from 6.5% in 2022 to 3.4% in 2023 to 2.9% in 2024; prices kept rising throughout ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/consumer-price-index-2025-in-review.htm)) |
| Deflation (in one item) | That item's price actually falls | Egg prices fell 20.9% and TV prices 7.2% in the 12 months to December 2025 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_01132026.htm)) |
| Deflation (economy-wide) | The overall price level falls | Rare in the US; the St. Louis Fed notes a slight bout in 2009 ([St. Louis Fed](https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/page-one-economics/2024/03/01/the-inflation-rate-is-falling-but-prices-are-not?print=true)) |

Notice that individual prices fall all the time, even when overall inflation is positive. The CPI is an average of hundreds of items, so a drop in eggs can be swamped by a rise in rent. That's the source of a lot of confusion: someone points out that gas got cheaper, someone else points out that their rent didn't, and both are right.

## Prices that did come down, and the ones that won't

### The ones that fell: eggs, gasoline and TVs

**Eggs** are the clearest case of a price that went up and came back down. According to BLS average price data (via FRED), a dozen grade A large eggs averaged [$1.535 in December 2019](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/APU0000708111), peaked at $6.227 in March 2025, and was back to $2.320 by October 2026. That's a drop of about 63% from the peak.

Even so, a dozen is still roughly 50% above the 2019 price. Prices that fall often settle above their old level rather than at it.

**Gasoline** swings in both directions because it tracks crude oil. The EIA reports that the national average for regular gas [hit $5.01 a gallon in June 2022](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=55099) and had fallen to $3.09 by the end of that year. It's well up again now: the EIA's weekly survey put regular at [$3.995 for the week of November 9, 2026](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/), $0.939 higher than a year earlier, and the BLS gasoline index was up [37.4% over the 12 months to October 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11102026.htm). Gas is the item most likely to fall back sharply, and also the most likely to jump again.

**TVs and electronics** have been getting cheaper for decades, partly because each year's model does more for the money and the CPI adjusts for that. The BLS TV index fell [7.2% in the 12 months to December 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_01132026.htm). Other durable goods can follow the same path: once the St. Louis Fed factors in better features and performance, some of them [end up costing less than they used to](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2025/july/differences-prices-inflation-explained).

### The ones that rarely fall: rent, insurance and other services

Services behave differently. The St. Louis Fed cites average annual inflation of [2.9% for services and 0.9% for goods](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2025/july/differences-prices-inflation-explained) between 1990 and 2024, and puts the gap down to productivity. Machines and better production lines let manufacturers turn out more for the same cost year after year, but a plumber still needs an afternoon to fix a leak and a nurse can only see so many patients in a shift. When wages rise, the cost of labor-heavy services rises with them, and wages very rarely go down.

The BLS figures show how this plays out over several years. Compounding the yearly December-to-December changes the BLS publishes, here's how much a few categories rose between December 2020 and December 2025 (a Guide Market calculation from [BLS data](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/consumer-price-index-2025-in-review.htm); small rounding differences are possible):

| Category | Change, Dec 2020 to Dec 2025 | Did it ever fall year over year? |
|---|---|---|
| Motor vehicle insurance | About +64% | No; it rose 20.3% in 2023 alone |
| Gasoline | About +35% | Yes, in 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025, but not enough to undo 2021's 49.6% jump |
| Shelter (rent and owners' housing costs) | About +28% | No |
| Food at home (groceries) | About +26% | No, though it rose only 1.3% in 2023 |
| All items | About +24% | No |

Gasoline is the interesting row. It fell four years running and still ended up well above where it started, because a single huge increase takes many small declines to reverse. Car insurance never fell at all. Premiums follow what it costs insurers to repair and replace cars, and repair costs are mostly parts and labor, the kind of costs that tend to ratchet up rather than reverse.

Rent behaves in a similar way. Leases typically reset once a year, landlords seldom cut rent for tenants who are staying, and the official shelter index reflects many leases signed at different times, so it moves slowly in either direction.

## Why it feels worse than the official number

Plenty of people feel the CPI understates what they're going through. Often they're right, and nobody has to be fudging the data for that to be true.

The first reason is that your basket isn't the average basket. The CPI weights each item by how much the average urban household spends on it. In September 2026, shelter made up [about 35% of the index](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11102026.htm), groceries about 8%, gasoline about 4% and car insurance about 2.5%. If you rent in an expensive city, drive a lot and have a young driver on your policy, your weights look nothing like that, and your inflation rate won't either.

Then there's how often you pay. Groceries and gas come up every week; a new TV comes up every few years. So the things that rose most (food, insurance, rent) are in front of you constantly, while the price falls happen in categories you rarely shop in.

Memory plays its part too. Most people compare today's price with what they remember paying before the 2021–2022 surge, not with last year's, which means they're judging the altitude rather than the speed.

A carton of eggs at $2.32 is "down 63%" if you remember March 2025, and "up 50%" if you remember 2019. Both are correct.

Renewals add a final twist, because they land in one lump. A 10% jump in rent or insurance hits all at once when the letter arrives, while the CPI spreads changes across the year. One bad renewal can make a whole year feel worse than the statistics suggest.

## Have wages caught up?

The honest answer is: on average, roughly yes, but it took years and the latest trend has turned the wrong way. That's what the BLS Real Earnings data, which adjusts average hourly pay for inflation, shows.

In November 2019, the BLS put average hourly earnings for all private-sector employees at [$28.29, or $10.97 in 1982–84 dollars](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/realer_12112019.htm) after adjusting for inflation. In October 2026, average hourly earnings were [$38.00, or $11.26 in 1982–84 dollars](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/realer_11102026.htm). So nominal pay rose by roughly a third, and after inflation average hourly pay was about 2.6% higher. The two figures come from releases published seven years apart, and BLS data get revised, so treat that comparison as approximate.

The more recent direction is less reassuring. Real hourly earnings rose [1.1% in the 12 months to December 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/realer_01132026.htm).

By August 2026, the 12-month change had turned negative, [down 0.3%](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/realer_09112026.htm), and by September it was down 0.5%, according to the [October 2026 release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/realer_11102026.htm). (There's no 12-month figure for October, for the shutdown-related reason explained below.) By late summer 2026, prices were running ahead of pay again.

Averages hide a lot. If you changed jobs or work in a field short of staff, your pay may have outrun prices comfortably. If you've been in the same role on a flat salary since 2020, it almost certainly hasn't. What matters is *your* pay against *your* costs, and the calculation further down helps with that.

## Why the Fed won't try to push prices back down

If prices rose too much, why doesn't the Federal Reserve just reverse it? Because getting the basket back to $100 would mean several years of deflation, and deflation tends to do more damage than the problem it fixes.

The Fed aims for inflation of [2% a year](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm), measured by the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, which it considers the rate most consistent with its twin goals of maximum employment and stable prices. The target is 2%, not zero and certainly not negative. "Stable prices" in the Fed's sense means low, predictable increases, not a frozen or falling price level.

The San Francisco Fed's explainer on deflation lays out [why falling prices are dangerous](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/doctor-econ/2003/05/deflation-risks/). Households that expect lower prices tomorrow hold off on big purchases today, and businesses facing that slack demand cut back on hiring. A mortgage or car loan doesn't shrink when prices do, but paychecks often do, so the same monthly payment takes a bigger bite out of income. And once interest rates hit zero, the central bank runs out of its usual tool for fighting a slump.

So the Fed's job, as it sees it, is to bring the *rate* back to 2% and keep it there, not to undo past increases. That's frustrating if you were hoping for 2019 prices. But the way there would likely run through job losses and pay cuts, and cheaper groceries don't help much if your paycheck shrinks or disappears. Our guide to [what a recession means for your money](/articles/what-a-recession-means-for-your-money) covers that side of the trade-off.

## How to read an inflation headline (and why October 2026 is an odd one)

Every month the BLS publishes several numbers, and headlines pick one. Knowing which is which saves a lot of confusion.

- **The monthly change** is how much prices moved in a single month, usually seasonally adjusted. It's noisy but timely.
- **The 12-month (annual) change** compares this month's index with the same month a year earlier. This is the number most headlines mean by "inflation is 3%".
- **Core inflation** strips out food and energy, which swing a lot, to show the underlying trend. It's useful for forecasting, but food and energy are things you still have to buy.

The October 2026 report, released on November 10, shows why you need to know the difference. Prices rose [0.4% in the month](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_11102026.htm), seasonally adjusted, and core prices rose 0.3%. Food rose 0.5%, energy 0.7% and shelter 0.3%, with shelter accounting for over 30% of the monthly increase.

What the report doesn't have is the headline annual rate. The BLS didn't collect survey data in October 2025 because of the lapse in federal funding, so for most indexes it has no October 2025 value to compare against, and its notice says it [cannot publish 12-month changes](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/notices/2026/12m-changes-202610.htm) for those series. A few series with October 2025 data, such as gasoline, do show a 12-month change.

>> **Tip:** When you read "inflation fell to X%," ask three questions. Is that monthly or annual? Is it all items or core? And is it the *rate* falling, or actual prices? Usually, it's the rate.

## How to work out your own inflation rate

The official CPI is an average. Your personal inflation rate is what happened to the cost of the things *you* buy. You can estimate it in about 30 minutes with your bank and card statements.

1. **Pick two periods a year apart.** Use the same month or quarter in each year (say, September 2025 and September 2026) to avoid seasonal differences like heating bills.
2. **List your regular spending categories.** Housing, groceries, fuel, insurance, utilities, phone and internet, childcare, and so on. Leave out one-off purchases like a new sofa.
3. **Write down what you spent on each in both periods.** Use statements, not memory.
4. **Adjust for changes in what you bought.** This is the step most people skip. If you spent more on groceries because you now feed an extra person, that's not inflation. Try to compare like with like: same rent for the same flat, same insurance cover, roughly the same shopping list.
5. **Add up both totals and compare.** Your personal inflation rate is the new total divided by the old total, minus one.

Here's an example with illustrative figures for a renter who drives to work (not real data):

| Category | Monthly cost, last year | Monthly cost, this year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent | $1,500 | $1,560 | +4.0% |
| Groceries | $600 | $630 | +5.0% |
| Gasoline | $200 | $260 | +30.0% |
| Car insurance | $150 | $165 | +10.0% |
| Utilities | $180 | $190 | +5.6% |
| Everything else | $870 | $885 | +1.7% |
| **Total** | **$3,500** | **$3,690** | **+5.4%** |

This household's personal inflation rate is about 5.4%. Look at where it comes from: gasoline is less than 6% of their spending, but its 30% jump adds $60 a month, nearly a third of the total increase. No national average can tell you that, and it shows you where to look first.

If tracking every category is too much, start with your three biggest: housing, food and transport. For most households they cover well over half of spending, and they're where the CPI weights most often differ from your own.

## What you can do about permanently higher prices

You can't make the price level fall. What you can control is the gap between your income and your costs, and there are more levers on both sides than most people use.

**Shop the items that do move.** Groceries, fuel and electronics are where prices actually fall and where substitutions work: store brands, buying staples in bulk when they're cheap, switching from beef to chicken when beef spikes (the BLS beef and veal index rose [16.4% in 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_01132026.htm)). Since you buy these weekly, small savings add up.

**Shop around for insurance at every renewal.** Car insurance rose the most of any category in the table above, and staying put tends to cost you. Get two or three quotes first, ask about a higher deductible if you have savings to cover it, and check that your cover still matches your car's value.

**Negotiate the rent before you accept a renewal.** Look up what comparable units nearby are listed for, and if your increase is above that, say so in writing. Landlords often prefer a reliable tenant at a smaller increase over an empty unit. It doesn't always work, but asking costs nothing.

**Make sure your savings earn more than inflation.** Cash in an account paying close to nothing loses value every year that inflation is positive. Compare your rate with other FDIC- or NCUA-insured accounts and move it if you're being underpaid. Money you might need in a crisis should stay where you can reach it within a day or two; [how to build an emergency fund](/articles/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund) covers which accounts fit.

If you live outside the US or buy imported goods, part of your price changes may also come from currency moves, which we cover in [how exchange rates affect your money](/articles/how-exchange-rates-affect-your-money).

**Ask for a raise with the numbers in hand.** This is the biggest lever, and the one most people leave alone. The CPI rose about 24% between December 2020 and December 2025, so a salary frozen since 2020 has lost roughly a fifth of its buying power. Say you've earned $50,000 the whole time: by the end of 2025 it bought about what $40,000 did in late 2020.

Bring that calculation, plus market pay data for your role, to the conversation. If your employer won't move, that's useful information about whether to look elsewhere.

>> **Common mistake:** waiting for prices to "normalize" before adjusting your budget. Most of them won't. Build your budget around today's prices, and treat any drop in eggs or gas as a bonus rather than a plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will prices ever go back down to 2019 levels?

For most things, no. Goods that spiked for specific reasons, like eggs or gas, can fall a long way, though eggs still settled about 50% above their 2019 price. Services such as rent and insurance almost never fall back, and the overall level would need years of deflation, which the Fed works to prevent.

### Is inflation going down right now?

Not clearly. US prices rose about 4.2% between December 2025 and October 2026 before seasonal adjustment, the October report showed a 0.4% monthly increase, and the Fed described inflation as elevated in October. The annual rate for October couldn't be published because of missing 2025 data. Check the latest [BLS CPI release](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/) for the current figure.

### What's the difference between disinflation and deflation?

In a sentence: disinflation is the climb slowing down, deflation is prices going down. The table in "Inflation, disinflation and deflation side by side" above sets them out with real US examples, including the rare economy-wide case.

### Why does my inflation feel higher than the CPI?

Probably because your spending isn't weighted like the average household's. If housing, insurance, groceries or fuel take a bigger share of your budget than the CPI assumes, your rate will be higher. The calculation above shows by how much.

## The takeaway

The 2021–2022 jump is baked in, and 2026 has added to it. Waiting it out isn't a plan. So set aside the question of when prices will come back down. The more useful one is whether your income is rising faster than your own costs.

This weekend, pull up last year's statements and spend half an hour working out your personal inflation rate. Then start with whichever renewal lands next, the insurance or the rent, and move any savings that pay next to nothing. If the numbers show your pay has slipped, take them to your next review.
