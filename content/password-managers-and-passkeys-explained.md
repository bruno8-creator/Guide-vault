Let's start with a confession that a lot of people share: you probably have two or three passwords that you vary slightly and use almost everywhere. Maybe one "strong" one for banking and a simpler one for everything else.

It's understandable. Nobody can remember a hundred unique passwords. But reusing them is the single biggest security risk most people have, and the fix is easier than it sounds.

## Why reusing passwords is the real problem

Websites get breached all the time, and lists of stolen email-and-password pairs end up circulating online. Attackers then run those pairs automatically against other sites — email providers, shops, banks, social networks. This is called **credential stuffing**, and it works precisely because people reuse passwords.

You can check whether your email address has appeared in known breaches with a service like [Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com). Many people are surprised by the results.

The takeaway is that a password doesn't need to be "cracked" if it's already been leaked from somewhere else. What protects you is having a *different* password on every site.

## What a password manager does

A password manager is an app that:

- generates long, random, unique passwords for each account,
- stores them in an encrypted vault protected by one main password,
- fills them in for you on websites and apps.

You remember one strong password. The manager remembers everything else.

Your phone and browser may already include one. Apple devices have a built-in password manager, as do Google Chrome and Android. Dedicated apps such as Bitwarden, 1Password and others work across different brands of devices, which matters if you use, say, an iPhone and a Windows laptop.

## "But isn't putting everything in one place risky?"

It's the most common objection, and it's reasonable. The honest comparison is between two imperfect options:

- **Without a manager:** weak or reused passwords, which are exposed every time any site you use is breached.
- **With a manager:** strong unique passwords, protected by one main password plus encryption, ideally with two-step verification on the account.

For almost everyone, the second is far safer. Reputable managers are designed so that even the company running the service can't read your vault.

## Passkeys: the next step

Passkeys are a newer way to sign in that replaces passwords entirely on sites that support them. Instead of typing a secret, your device proves who you are using a cryptographic key, unlocked with your fingerprint, face or device PIN.

The practical benefits:

- There's no password to steal or reuse.
- They can't be phished: a passkey only works on the real website it was created for, so a fake login page can't capture it.
- Signing in is usually faster — a quick fingerprint or face scan.

Passkeys are stored in your password manager or your device's built-in keychain and sync across your devices. Support is growing across major services, but not every site offers them yet, which is why you'll still need a password manager for the rest.

## An afternoon plan

You don't have to fix every account at once. This order gets you most of the benefit quickly:

1. **Choose a manager** and install it on your phone and computer. Use the built-in one if all your devices are from the same ecosystem; otherwise pick a cross-platform app.
2. **Create a strong main password.** A good approach is four or five random unrelated words. Write it down and keep it somewhere safe at home while you learn it.
3. **Turn on two-step verification** for the password manager itself.
4. **Secure your email first.** Your email can reset every other password, so it's the most important account you have. Give it a new unique password and two-step verification, or a passkey if offered.
5. **Then do money and identity accounts:** banks, payment apps, mobile phone account, government services.
6. **Fix the rest as you go.** Each time you log in somewhere with an old password, let the manager generate a new one and save it.

Within a few weeks, most of your important accounts will have unique passwords without you having to sit through a marathon session.

## A few common mistakes to avoid

- **Don't skip the recovery setup.** Most managers offer a recovery code or emergency access. Set it up and store the code somewhere physical and safe.
- **Avoid SMS codes where better options exist.** Text-message codes are better than nothing, but authenticator apps and passkeys are stronger.
- **Don't save your main password in the browser** of a shared computer.

## The takeaway

You don't need to become a security expert. You need one good main password, a password manager to handle the rest, and passkeys wherever they're offered. An afternoon of setup removes the biggest risk most people carry around without thinking about it.
