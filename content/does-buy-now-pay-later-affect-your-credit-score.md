You're at checkout on Black Friday with a $400 order in the basket, and under the total there's a little button: four interest-free payments of $100. It looks harmless. Then you remember that you hope to apply for a mortgage next year, and you wonder: does buy now, pay later (BNPL) affect your credit score, and will a future lender ever see this loan?

The honest answer is that it depends on three things most articles mix up. One is what the BNPL company sends to the credit bureaus, which varies by company and even by product. Another is which score the lender actually uses: most scores in use today ignore BNPL data even when it's sitting on your report. The third, and the one people forget, is what a lender can see outside your report altogether, starting with your bank statements.

Those three can give three different answers about the same purchase. Below you'll find what each major provider says on its own website (checked on November 16, 2026), a missed payment played out on that $400 order, and what it all means if a mortgage is on your horizon.

>> **In short:** Paid on time, a pay-in-4 plan rarely touches the scores lenders use today; the trouble starts with missed payments, collections and what an underwriter spots in your bank statements.

>> - Reporting differs by provider. [Affirm](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250319542289/en/Affirm-Expands-Credit-Reporting-with-Experian-to-Include-All-Pay-Over-Time-Products) reports all its loans, Pay in 4 included, to Experian and [TransUnion](https://investors.affirm.com/news-releases/news-release-details/affirm-expands-credit-reporting-transunion-all-pay-over-time). ## Does buy now, pay later affect your credit score? Three different questions

When people ask whether BNPL "affects your credit", they usually mean "will my score go down?". That's only one of three questions, and it's the one with the least worrying answer.

**Is it reported?** A credit report is the file kept by Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, and BNPL companies don't have to send data to them. [Experian notes](https://www.experian.com/blogs/insights/what-bnpl-rules-reveal-about-the-future-of-mortgage-credit-scoring/) that BNPL reporting isn't mandated under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, although some providers have started doing it voluntarily.

**Is it scored?** Even when a BNPL loan is on your report, the scoring model has to be built to read it. [TransUnion explains](https://www.transunion.com/blog/bnpl-and-point-of-sale-lending) that it tags BNPL loans as their own category, keeps them in a separate section of the file and leaves them out of its default deliveries and of scores that aren't built for BNPL. So a standard score doesn't see them unless the model is designed to use that data.

**Is it visible some other way?** A mortgage underwriter reads your bank statements and asks about your debts, and a string of $100 payments to four BNPL apps turns up there whatever your score says.

If you want a refresher on how the scoring side works in general, our guide to [how credit scores work and how to improve yours](/articles/how-credit-scores-work-and-how-to-improve-yours) covers the basics.

## Pay in 4 vs monthly financing: two very different products

"Buy now, pay later" covers two kinds of loan, and most of the confusion online comes from treating them as one.

**Pay-in-4 plans** split a purchase into four equal parts. You usually pay the first at checkout and the rest every two weeks. PayPal's version, for example, is [interest-free, runs a little over six weeks](https://www.paypal.com/us/cshelp/article/HELP463) and works on carts between $10 and $2,000. These are the small, short loans most people mean by BNPL.

**Monthly financing** spreads a larger purchase over months, often with interest. Afterpay calls its version Pay Monthly, PayPal has Pay Monthly, and Klarna has a product it calls a Term Loan in the US. These look much more like a traditional instalment loan, and providers treat them differently on credit checks and reporting.

One rule of thumb holds up across providers: **the longer and bigger the loan, the more likely it is to involve a fuller credit check and to show up on your credit report.** Pay-in-4 plans are where the providers differ most.

>> **Tip:** Before you confirm, look at the name of the plan on the checkout screen. "Pay in 4" and "Pay Monthly" from the same company can be treated very differently, so the product name tells you which column of the table below applies to you.

I checked each provider's own website on November 16, 2026, and used only what the provider itself says. Third-party guides contradict each other, so where a provider's site doesn't say something, the table says so instead of guessing. Providers change their policies, so it's worth reading your own loan agreement too.

A few things stand out.

**Affirm is the most open about reporting.** It now sends all its pay-over-time loans, including Pay in 4, to two of the three bureaus. Its own [shopper explainer](https://businesshub.affirm.com/hc/en-us/articles/5999202737172-What-is-Affirm) says your payment plan and repayment activity may be reported, and that this can affect your score.

When Affirm announced the Experian change, it said the Pay in 4 data [wouldn't be included in traditional credit scores for now](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250319542289/en/Affirm-Expands-Credit-Reporting-with-Experian-to-Include-All-Pay-Over-Time-Products) but would appear on your Experian file. Put simply: on time or late, an Affirm Pay in 4 loan can sit on your report.

**Klarna's split is by product.** Its US announcement was explicit that Pay in 4, Pay in 30 days and Pay Now were not part of the reporting, while Term Loans would be, positive and negative alike. That release dates from 2024 and is still the latest official US statement I found, so check the terms shown at checkout.

**Afterpay and Zip say they don't report in the US.** Afterpay adds that it has no plans to start until BNPL data is shown to help customers' scores. That doesn't mean a missed payment has no consequences, as the late-payment section below shows.

**PayPal is the least clear.** Its Pay in 4 pages cover the application, not the repayments. Its Pay Monthly page warns that late or missed payments may be reported. If this matters to you, read the agreement before you accept.

Sezzle isn't in the table because I only list providers whose policy I could confirm on their own site. Equifax doesn't appear in any provider statement I could verify.

## Does applying for BNPL mean a hard credit check?

For the pay-in-4 products covered here, the providers describe soft checks. A soft check lets a company look at your file without the inquiry counting against your score.

- **PayPal:** a soft credit check [may be needed and won't affect your score](https://www.paypal.com/us/cshelp/article/questions-about-pay-in-4-applications-help464). PayPal also says it may use external bureau data to assess you.
- **Affirm:** opening an account or seeing what you qualify for is treated as [a soft inquiry](https://businesshub.affirm.com/hc/en-us/articles/5999202737172-What-is-Affirm). Affirm may pass it to the bureaus, but it leaves your credit untouched.
- **Afterpay:** may run [a soft check when you first sign up](https://www.afterpay.com/en-US/help/4487846176793-Does-Afterpay-conduct-credit-checks), and runs a credit check when you choose Pay Monthly. The page doesn't say whether that second check is soft or hard.
- **Zip:** uses [a soft check when you apply](https://zip.co/us/zip-insights/does-using-bnpl-affect-your-credit-score), so even a declined application doesn't affect your score.
- **Klarna:** I couldn't confirm Klarna's US credit-check policy on its own website, so check the terms at checkout.

Two caveats. A soft inquiry can still appear on the copy of your report that you see, even though lenders don't see it and it doesn't count. And a "no" from a BNPL app tells you nothing about your score. Providers decide on their own data, and PayPal says plainly that [you may be approved for only one product or none](https://www.paypal.com/us/cshelp/article/questions-about-pay-in-4-applications-help464) depending on your creditworthiness.

## Which credit scores can see BNPL, and do lenders use them?

Here the reporting question and the scoring question come apart.

**FICO Score 8.** FICO calls it the [version most widely used by lenders](https://myfico.com/legal/fico-score-versions). On its BNPL FAQ, myFICO explains that [no bureau is passing BNPL account data into the way FICO Scores are worked out at the moment](https://www.myfico.com/credit-education/faq/scores/how-does-BNPL-impact-score), which means your BNPL accounts don't count toward your FICO Score. The page is undated and frames this as the current situation, which is fair: it could change.

**FICO Score 10 BNPL and 10 T BNPL.** FICO [announced these in June 2025](https://investors.fico.com/news-releases/news-release-details/fico-unveils-groundbreaking-credit-scores-incorporate-buy-now) as its first scores built to include BNPL data, with launch planned for autumn 2025, offered alongside existing versions at no extra fee.

One detail matters for holiday shoppers. FICO found that people often open several BNPL loans in a short window, so the models group separate BNPL loans together for some calculations. FICO says this raises scores for some BNPL users, since a burst of small loans isn't treated the same way as several separate new accounts.

**Who actually uses the new scores?** That's the open question. Lenders choose which score to buy, and switching models costs them money and system changes. In a [September 2025 Digital Transactions article](https://www.digitaltransactions.net/it-could-be-years-before-bnpl-data-have-any-real-impact-on-lending-decisions/), a credit-risk executive at LexisNexis Risk Solutions predicted gradual adoption, and a FICO spokesperson said the pace would vary across the industry. I found no public figures on how many lenders have adopted the BNPL versions.

**VantageScore.** The FHFA has [allowed VantageScore 4.0 on all Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac mortgages since July 8, 2025](https://vantagescore.com/resources/knowledge-center/press_releases/vantagescore-4-0-allowed-for-use-on-all-fannie-mae-and-freddie-mac-mortgages-effective-immediately), which matters for home buyers. VantageScore has said in public that paying BNPL loans as agreed [could help millions of people's scores](https://www.vantagescore.com/resources/knowledge-center/could-your-buy-now-pay-later-loans-affect-your-credit-score-heres-what-you-need-to-know-cnbc), but I couldn't find an official VantageScore statement that its current models score BNPL data. Given TransUnion's own description of keeping BNPL tradelines out of default deliveries, I'd assume most scores you'll meet in the next year don't use it.

If you're a home buyer, the mortgage scores are what count. FICO lists [Scores 2, 4 and 5](https://myfico.com/legal/fico-score-versions) as the versions used in mortgage lending, all older than FICO 8. Experian notes that it [offers FICO 2 and VantageScore 4.0](https://www.experian.com/blogs/insights/what-bnpl-rules-reveal-about-the-future-of-mortgage-credit-scoring/) on mortgage files.

| Score | Can it use BNPL data? | Where you're likely to meet it |
|---|---|---|
| FICO Score 8 | No, per myFICO, because the bureaus don't feed BNPL into FICO Score calculations | Most lenders' general decisions |
| FICO Scores 2, 4, 5 | No, per the same myFICO statement | Mortgages |
| FICO Score 10 BNPL / 10 T BNPL | Yes, built for it | Lenders who choose to adopt it; adoption figures not public |
| VantageScore 4.0 | No official confirmation found | Apps and some lenders; accepted for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac mortgages |

**Today, paying your Affirm loan on time mostly builds a record on your report rather than a higher number.** Missing payments is a different story, because a seriously overdue debt can come back to you through collections.

## What happens if you miss a payment: a $400 holiday example

This is an illustrative example. It uses each provider's published rules, but your own agreement is what counts.

You buy $400 of gifts on Black Friday, November 27, 2026, and choose a pay-in-4 plan: $100 at checkout, then $100 roughly every two weeks, around December 11, December 25 and January 8. The second payment goes through. The third falls on Christmas Day, when your checking account is short after the holidays, and the automatic debit fails.

**The day it fails.** Most providers take payments automatically from the card or bank account you chose. A failed payment may also bring a returned-payment or NSF fee from your own bank. PayPal points this out even though it charges [no late or NSF fees of its own](https://www.paypal.com/us/cshelp/article/HELP463).

**Within days.** What happens next depends on the provider:

- **Afterpay** [stops you making new purchases as soon as you miss a payment](https://www.afterpay.com/en-US/help/360016052892-How-do-payments-work). It can charge a late fee of [up to $8 per missed instalment](https://www.afterpay.com/en-US/help/900005359783-Is-there-a-cost-to-using-Afterpay), with all late fees on an order capped at 25% of its value, which would be $100 on this order. Afterpay says it doesn't report to US bureaus.
- **PayPal Pay in 4** charges no late fee, but the loan [can become past due](https://www.paypal.com/us/cshelp/article/questions-about-pay-in-4-repayments---help1099), you may get automated calls and reminders, and missed repayments may affect whether you can use Pay Later again.
- **Affirm** says it [charges no late fees](https://businesshub.affirm.com/hc/en-us/articles/5999202737172-What-is-Affirm). Because it reports Pay in 4 loans to Experian and TransUnion, your repayment history, late payments included, can end up on those files.

**At 30 days past due.** For lenders that report, this is the usual threshold. [Experian explains](https://experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/when-do-late-payments-get-reported) that lenders don't usually flag a payment to the bureaus as late until a full 30 days have passed since it was due. So a payment a few days late is unlikely to reach your report. One that drifts past a month can, if your provider reports that product.

**If it's still unpaid weeks later.** None of the provider pages I checked set out exactly when an unpaid BNPL order is charged off or sent to a debt collector, and your agreement will spell that out. If you only manage one thing in this whole timeline, make it not getting here. A collection account is a serious negative mark in its own right, and it doesn't depend on whether the original BNPL loan was ever reported. Our [credit scores guide](/articles/how-credit-scores-work-and-how-to-improve-yours) explains how long that kind of entry can stay on your report.

>> **What to do the moment a BNPL payment fails:**
>> - Log in to the app the same day and pay the instalment with another card or account. Afterpay, for one, [lets you pay by a different method](https://www.afterpay.com/en-US/help/900005359783-Is-there-a-cost-to-using-Afterpay) after it notifies you of a failed payment.
>> - If you can't pay, contact the provider before the next due date and ask about moving it.
>> - Check every other BNPL plan that will debit the same account in the next few weeks, so one failure doesn't knock over the next.

## Loan stacking: the real holiday risk

The CFPB's research points at a quieter problem than any single late payment. In a [January 2025 report](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_BNPL_Report_2025_01.pdf) based on data from major BNPL lenders, it looked at how often people took out a new BNPL loan while another was still being repaid.

```chart

unit: %
source: CFPB, Consumer Use of Buy Now, Pay Later and Other Unsecured Debt (January 2025) | https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_BNPL_Report_2025_01.pdf
note: The CFPB counts loans as simultaneous when a new BNPL loan is taken out within 42 days of an earlier one.
At any BNPL lender: 62.7
At two or more different BNPL lenders: 32.6
```

About six in ten borrowers had overlapping loans, and about a third had them with more than one company. That second number matters because no single app can see the whole picture. Each one checks your ability to pay its own plan, not the three others debiting the same account in December.

Here's how that plays out. Say that alongside the $400 Black Friday order you take a $240 plan at a second app the following week and a $600 plan at a third in mid-December. Each instalment looks small on its own: $100, $60, $150. But if the due dates bunch together just before Christmas, more than $300 leaves your account within a few days, and none of the three apps knows about the other two. That's a hypothetical, but it's exactly the overlap the CFPB is counting.

The same report found that BNPL borrowers carried noticeably higher credit card utilisation than people never seen using BNPL. Its authors are careful to say this is a correlation, not proof that BNPL causes it. Still, if your cards are already near their limits, adding pay-in-4 plans on top is the pattern to watch.

## BNPL and a mortgage application

If you're planning to buy a home next year, this is the section that matters most, and it isn't really about your score.

Mortgage lenders work out your debt-to-income ratio. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, the rulebook for loans sold to Fannie Mae, says lenders must get [full disclosure of all credit obligations](https://www.fanniemae.com/content/guide/selling/b3/6/08.html) from borrowers, including liabilities that don't appear on the credit report.

It also says instalment debts with ten or fewer monthly payments left are counted [if the payments significantly affect your ability to meet your obligations](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-02/debt-income-ratios). Neither page mentions BNPL by name. In practice, the underwriter decides how to treat it.

>> **Watch out:** your bank statements show BNPL debits even when your credit report doesn't. Recurring payments to Affirm, Klarna or Afterpay in the months before you apply can prompt questions, and you'll be expected to explain them.

The good news is that pay-in-4 plans end quickly. A plan that started on Black Friday is usually finished by mid-January, so if you're applying in spring, it will probably be paid off and behind you. **What draws attention is opening new plans in the run-up to an application**, for the same reason people are often told not to open new credit cards before a mortgage. Several active plans at once is exactly the pattern an underwriter notices.

## BNPL vs a credit card for holiday shopping

The fair comparison isn't "BNPL bad, cards good". A card you pay in full each month is free credit with strong legal protections. A card you carry a balance on is usually the most expensive option in this article. Our guide to [how Fed rate changes affect you](/articles/how-fed-interest-rate-changes-affect-you) explains why card rates stay high.

| | Pay-in-4 BNPL | Credit card paid in full | Credit card carrying a balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest | None on the pay-in-4 products covered here | None if paid by the due date | Card APR on the balance |
| Late fees | Varies: none at PayPal and Affirm; up to $8 per instalment at Afterpay | Card's late fee | Card's late fee |
| Credit check to use it | Usually soft | Already approved | Already approved |
| Effect on most scores today | Usually none, unless it goes to collections | Reported every month; balance affects utilisation | Reported; high balances can lower scores |
| Legal dispute rights | Provider's own policies | Fair Credit Billing Act | Fair Credit Billing Act |

The last row is the one people miss. With a credit card, the Fair Credit Billing Act lets you dispute billing errors in writing [within 60 days of the first bill that shows the error](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/using-credit-cards-and-disputing-charges). That covers items that weren't delivered as agreed, and federal law also caps your liability for unauthorised card charges at $50.

For BNPL, the CFPB issued an interpretive rule in 2024 saying many BNPL lenders should give similar rights. It [withdrew that rule on May 12, 2025](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/consumer-cards-resources/buy-now-pay-later-bnpl-products/). If a gift never arrives, you're relying on the BNPL provider's own dispute process.

My view: for a holiday budget you can genuinely cover over six weeks, pay-in-4 is a reasonable tool, especially from a provider with no late fees. For anything you can't cover in six weeks, neither BNPL nor a card balance is the answer. A smaller holiday is, along with some money set aside before next year. Our guide to [building an emergency fund](/articles/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund) shows how to start.

## A checklist before you tap "pay in 4"

1. **Add up every plan you already have.** Open each BNPL app and write down every payment due in the next eight weeks, with dates. That's your real December and January.
2. **Check the dates against payday.** If a payment lands two days before your pay arrives, it's the one most likely to fail.
3. **Read which product you're choosing.** Pay in 4 and monthly financing are treated differently on credit checks, interest and reporting, even at the same provider.
4. **Know the late-fee rule.** None, a flat fee or a capped fee, and how long the grace period is.
5. **Pay from a debit account with a buffer, or a card you pay in full.** Paying BNPL with a credit card you then carry a balance on gets you the costs of both.
6. **Keep your order confirmations.** If something goes wrong with delivery, you'll need them for the provider's dispute process.
7. **Mortgage or car loan in the next six months?** Many people skip new BNPL plans, or make sure they'll be paid off well before they apply.
8. **Set a reminder two days before each payment,** even with autopay on.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Klarna affect your credit score?

According to Klarna's 2024 US announcement, Pay in 4, Pay in 30 days and Pay Now aren't shared with credit bureaus, while Term Loans opened from November 2024 are shared with TransUnion, including missed payments. Klarna said at the time that this data wouldn't affect FICO or VantageScore scores for now. Check the terms at checkout, since that announcement is from 2024.

### Does Affirm report to credit bureaus?

Yes. Affirm reports all its pay-over-time loans, Pay in 4 included, to Experian (loans from April 1, 2025) and TransUnion (from May 1, 2025). Whether that data affects a score depends on the model a lender uses. Most versions in use today don't score it.

### Does Afterpay affect your credit score?

Afterpay says it doesn't currently report to US credit bureaus, and that its soft checks don't affect your score. A missed payment can still bring late fees and blocks new purchases until you catch up.

### Can BNPL help me build credit?

Not much yet. Even when on-time payments are reported, the most widely used scores don't count them. Models like FICO Score 10 BNPL may change that if lenders adopt them. For building credit today, a card you pay in full every month is the more reliable route.

### Will a BNPL purchase show up when I apply for a mortgage?

It may appear on your credit report if your provider reports it, and it'll show in your bank statements regardless. Under Fannie Mae's rules, lenders must get full disclosure of all your debts, so having plans paid off before you apply keeps things simpler.

## The takeaway

For most people, the score is the smaller worry. Before you tap the button this holiday season, spend ten minutes on the boring part: open every BNPL app you use, write the due dates next to your paydays, and read the name of the plan you're about to pick. If a mortgage or car loan is coming in the next six months, settle whether you want any new plans at all before you're standing at checkout.

If the dates and the amounts fit your budget, BNPL is a reasonable tool. If they don't, the button won't fix that.
